Store Life Jackets for Winter: Dry, Inspect, Separate Foam From Inflatable

Winter storage is not stuffing every vest into one tote. Dry first, inspect by construction, quarantine defects, protect foam from compression and make every inflatable model prove its own ready status.

By Nautical Dream Editorial DeskUpdated October 16, 202614 minute read

A life jacket can look tidy in a sealed bin and still emerge with mildew, compressed flotation, corroded hardware or an inflatable mechanism that is not ready. The U.S. Coast Guard's care guidance calls for thorough drying, ventilation and storage away from direct heat; current manufacturer guidance for inflatables adds model-specific inspection, leak-test, rearming and repacking steps. Those are two different branches. Do not apply a foam-jacket wash routine to an inflator, and do not treat a green status window as the complete inspection.

Start with the label and manual

Photograph the approval, care and model labels before cleaning. Record adult/child size, intended wearer, construction type, manufacturer, model, serial or lot information if present, purchase or in-service date if known, and the exact manual location. For an inflatable, record the inflator family, compatible rearm-kit number and any replacement or service date printed on the device.

Do not copy parts or intervals from a jacket that looks similar. Inflator indicators, cylinders, bobbins, hydrostatic mechanisms, folding patterns and tests differ. The Coast Guard notes that a rearming kit must match the specific inflatable. If the model, kit or manual cannot be identified, contact the manufacturer or retire the unit rather than guessing.

Clean only by construction-specific instructions

The Coast Guard's published cleaning guidance says an inflatable should be hand-washed or sponged with warm soapy water without submerging the inflator, then rinsed and hung to dry; inherently buoyant foam devices may be fully submerged under that guidance. It also warns against dry cleaning, chlorine bleach and direct heat. The product label and maker remain controlling.

Remove loose dirt with a soft brush and use only the cleaner, water temperature and immersion method the label permits. Do not put a life jacket in a washer or dryer unless the maker explicitly allows it. Do not use solvent, fuel, disinfectant, pressure washing or a stiff abrasive brush. Oil or chemical contamination that cannot be removed by the approved method is a manufacturer decision, not a cosmetic challenge.

Dry completely without cooking the materials

Open closures as the manual permits, spread layers for airflow and hang the jacket in a ventilated place out of direct sunlight. Do not place it on a radiator, aim a space heater at it, use a clothes dryer or leave it against a hot engine-room surface. Heat can damage foam, coatings, adhesives, bladder seams and inflation components.

Surface dryness is not enough. Check folds, webbing, pockets, collar, foam edges and the inflatable's permitted interior areas. Turn or reposition according to the label and allow more time when humidity is high. A calendar deadline is not proof. Store only when touch, smell and concealed folds indicate the device is fully dry.

Inspect foam jackets without compressing them

Look for torn fabric, exposed or shifted foam, permanent creases, hard or crumbling sections, mildew, oil stains, broken buckles, frozen zippers, frayed webbing and failed stitching. Do not kneel on the jacket, use it as a seat cushion, hang heavy gear from it or stack a tote on top. The Coast Guard's care guidance specifically warns against using a PFD as a kneeling pad or fender and calls for ventilated storage.

Do not open a seam to inspect or replace foam, add patches, paint the jacket, sew on unofficial accessories or move straps. Alteration can make a device unserviceable. If flotation is compressed, components are damaged, the label is unreadable or there is doubt about serviceability, quarantine it and consult the manufacturer or replace it.

Inspect inflatables by exact model

Start with the owner's manual. Mustang's current general guide calls for a visual check before use and more detailed periodic inspections, but explicitly says details vary by style. Inspect the shell, stitching, webbing, buckles, oral tube, dust cap, pull tab, closures and any status window. Check the cylinder and other components only as that manual directs.

Do not assume green means complete, and never install a cartridge based on thread appearance or gas weight alone. Corrosion, a pierced cylinder, expired component, failed indicator or wrong kit is a stop. Do not test-fire a cylinder for reassurance. Oral inflation, retention testing, deflation and repacking must follow the exact sequence and duration in the model manual; if the bladder loses firmness or the result is unclear, contact the maker or an authorized service provider.

Mustang's current storage guidance recommends a cool, dry, dark location, drying first, avoiding damp lockers and car trunks, not sealing the PFD in a plastic bag and keeping heavy objects off it. Follow the exact product directions if they differ. Maintain airflow and protect devices from pests, sharp edges, chemicals and sunlight.

Do not leave the winter inventory aboard

A boat stored outdoors, shrink-wrapped, covered or unheated can trap moisture and experience large temperature swings. The Coast Guard advises not leaving a PFD aboard for long periods when the boat is not in use. Move the inventory to a dry controlled location unless the manufacturer approves the onboard environment.

Removing the jackets is not permission to leave the boat without its legally required equipment during any operation. If the boat may be used during winter, maintain a separate ready set appropriate for the conditions and every passenger. Cold water increases consequences; the cold-weather fit guide helps verify that jackets still fit over actual layers.

Run the spring release inspection

  1. Retrieve the record and exact manual; do not rely on memory.
  2. Inspect label, shell, flotation, straps, hardware and contamination under bright light.
  3. Complete the model-specific inflatable inspection, test and repack steps.
  4. Assign each jacket to a current wearer and intended activity.
  5. Perform the maker's dry fit test with all normal clothing layers.
  6. Check the boat count, throwable device and legal/activity requirements for the current trip.
  7. Record the release date and keep quarantined gear physically separate.

Children grow, adults change layers and guests change the count. A jacket that passed in October is not automatically a March fit. Connect the release to the life-jacket buying guide and predeparture safety checklist.

What not to buy for storage

Skip vacuum bags, sealed plastic totes without a proven dry environment, space-saving compression sacks, wire hangers that can crease material, cedar oils or moth chemicals near fabric, and “universal” inflatable rearm kits. A large open shelf or wide hanger can be the better purchase because it preserves shape and makes inspection obvious.

Do not buy a replacement part until the exact model and compatible kit are verified from the label and manufacturer. When to replace or get qualified help

Replace or obtain manufacturer evaluation when the label or approval cannot be read, fit is wrong, foam is crushed or damaged, fabric or webbing is torn, closures fail, contamination persists, an inflatable leaks, the correct rearm kit is unavailable, the inflator status cannot be confirmed, or a component is corroded, expired or incompatible. Do not donate a failed life jacket as usable safety gear.

The storage rule is blunt: clean by the label, dry without heat, inspect by construction, quarantine uncertainty, store without compression and make every jacket earn a spring release. A neat bin is not evidence of readiness.

Frequently asked questions

Can I store life jackets in a sealed plastic tote?

Only if the exact manufacturer permits it and the environment is proven dry. Current manufacturer guidance warns that plastic bags can trap moisture; ventilation and no compression are safer defaults.

Can foam life jackets be stacked?

Do not place heavy objects on them or compress the foam. Store them with enough room to preserve shape and follow the product label.

Should I remove the CO2 cylinder from an inflatable life jacket for winter?

Follow the exact model manual. Do not remove, replace or test components based on generic advice, and use only the specified rearm kit.

Does a green indicator mean an inflatable life jacket is ready?

Not by itself. The shell, bladder, oral tube, webbing, closures, cylinder and model-specific inspection requirements also matter.
