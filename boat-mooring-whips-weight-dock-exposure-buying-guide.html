Mooring whips solve one narrow problem: they use flexible poles and lines to hold a boat away from a dock or seawall while ordinary mooring lines still control the boat. They do not strengthen a weak dock, erase a severe wake climate or turn a temporary tie-up into unattended storm storage. The correct buying decision is a system decision—boat, bases, structure, geometry, lines and exposure—not a choice between the longest two poles on a product page.

Define the job before shopping

Record whether the boat will be attended for an afternoon, kept at the dock overnight or left for a season. Add hull type, actual loaded weight, beam, freeboard, canopy or tower, intended stand-off distance, bottom clearance and normal bow and stern line arrangement. A light runabout on a protected inland cove is a different engineering job from a high-sided cabin boat on an exposed seawall.

Write the worst routine wake and wind direction, not the calm photograph used to sell the slip. Note current, water-level range, ice, debris and whether the dock floats or remains fixed. If the system must survive a named storm, flood, seasonal drawdown or freeze, ask the manufacturer and dock professional for that duty explicitly; do not infer it from a recreational weight table.

Use loaded weight before overall length

Taylor Made's current BoatGuard page says model weight is more important than length and warns that some docking conditions may be too rugged for the product. Its standard and premium table also pairs pole length with boat-weight ranges. Those are manufacturer-specific selection boundaries, not universal ratings that can be transferred to another brand.

Use the boat builder's dry weight only as a starting point. Add engine, batteries, installed options, fuel, water, gear and anything normally left aboard. Do not guess at an undocumented older hull. A travel lift ticket or certified scale can resolve uncertainty better than a forum estimate. If loaded weight approaches a stated limit, ask the whip maker whether the application is acceptable instead of treating the last pound as spare capacity.

Inspect the dock before choosing the base

A whip transfers recurring bending and line loads into its base and the dock framing. Surface boards alone do not prove that the mounting location is sound. Identify joists, stringers, blocking, fasteners, edge distance, rot, corrosion and movement below the deck. A decorative cap, thin aluminum skin or loose plank is not automatically a mounting structure.

Use only the maker's current base, backing and fastener instructions for the exact model. When the structure is unknown, deteriorated, cantilevered or shared with utilities, stop and use a qualified dock builder or marine contractor. Never drill where fuel, electrical, water or structural components may be hidden. The chafe-guard guide helps with contact protection, but no sleeve repairs a weak base.

Prove the geometry on paper

Sketch the boat parallel to the dock. Mark the two approved boat attachment points, each base position, the maker's required pole angle and the stand-off needed to keep hull, rail, rub strake, engine and swim platform clear through routine movement. Confirm the poles can be raised, lowered or stored without striking a canopy, overhead wire, neighbor's boat or walking path.

Do not attach a whip line to a windshield frame, rail stanchion or convenience handle unless the boat builder identifies it for the load. Do not let lines cross the boarding route or a propeller. The system must still allow a controlled pull-in for boarding without trapping fingers between hull and dock. Use docking-equipment basics and dock-line geometry to keep the stand-off system separate from the boat's primary restraint.

Keep conventional mooring lines in the design

Whip lines create outward stand-off; conventional bow, stern and spring lines control fore-and-aft travel and keep the boat from escaping the berth. Follow both the whip maker and boat builder for attachment points and tension. A photo showing only two bent poles is not a complete mooring plan.

Rig enough slack for expected water-level movement without allowing the hull to reach the dock. Recheck after the first wake cycle and again after wind direction changes. Fenders remain useful during boarding and any controlled pull-in, but they are a backup contact layer, not evidence that the geometry is correct. The fender guide covers size and placement without pretending fenders replace separation.

Choose among fixed, rocker and heavier-duty categories

Fixed-angle systems are simple and often fit smaller boats in moderate-wake water when the published weight and site limits agree. Rocker or pivoting bases can move poles upright when released and are offered by some makers for heavier or higher-profile boats. Heavy-duty systems may use larger-diameter poles and different bases, but “heavy duty” is not a cross-brand engineering standard.

Compare documented boat weight, pole length, pole diameter, base type, material, replacement-part support and stated environment. Reject listings that provide only a boat-length claim, omit installed hardware or mix photographs from several models. Also reject a kit whose line, cleat or base capacity is not documented for the same system.

Commission the system in controlled conditions

Install only by the current instructions or use a qualified professional. Before leaving the boat unattended, test in calm water with a spotter: pull the boat in, board, release it, observe both poles, check line travel and confirm the hull remains clear. Increase exposure gradually while someone can intervene from shore. Never stand in a loaded line's snap-back path.

Mark the working line settings and photograph the correct rig for the household. After the first day, inspect base fasteners, backing, poles, tips, lines, cleats and boat attachment points. Stop for cracking, permanent pole bend, loose hardware, fastener movement, cut fibers, chafe or a changed dock surface. A qualified marine technician or dock professional should evaluate damage and undocumented modifications.

Build a monthly and storm-removal rule

Create a card with system model, boat loaded weight, approved line settings, inspection points, maker contact and the weather threshold at which the boat leaves the berth. Inspect after major wakes, strong winds, water-level change or any contact event. Replace parts by the exact maker schedule and instructions; UV exposure and repeated flexing are not visible from the house.

A mooring whip is not a hurricane plan, ice plan or unattended flood plan unless the maker explicitly documents that duty for the installed system. When the forecast exceeds the approved use, remove the boat early enough to do so safely. The family weather gate and end-of-season checklist help define those exits.

Frequently asked questions

Should I size boat mooring whips by length or weight?

Use the exact manufacturer table. Taylor Made specifically says model weight is more important than length for its BoatGuard line, and its other current tables pair both boat size and weight with each model.

Do mooring whips replace bow and stern lines?

No. Whip lines provide stand-off while conventional mooring lines still control the boat. Follow the exact system and boat instructions.

Can I install mooring whips on any dock?

No. The bases transfer load into the dock structure. Unknown, damaged or lightly built structures need evaluation by the maker and a qualified dock professional before drilling or loading.
