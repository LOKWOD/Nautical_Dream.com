Raft-Up Safety and Etiquette

A raft-up works because several boats temporarily behave like one floating system. The group needs one plan for anchors, loads, movement, weather and departure—not ten separate assumptions tied together with dock lines.

Choose a location that can carry the whole group

Evaluate depth, bottom, swing room, wake exposure, wind shift, traffic and local restrictions for the combined raft rather than the first boat alone. A cove that is comfortable for one boat may become cramped when beam, freeboard and turning radius multiply.

Designate an anchor boat with suitable ground tackle, experienced crew and enough deck hardware. Depending on conditions and local practice, additional anchors may be appropriate, but multiple anchors create their own retrieval and entanglement problems. Agree on the arrangement before the second boat arrives.

Build from the center outward

The anchor boat should be settled and its position confirmed before others come alongside. Similar hull sizes and freeboards are easier to pair. Place heavier or taller boats where their movement will not overpower a much smaller neighbor.

Approaching boats should rig fenders and lines before entering the raft. Use bow, stern and spring lines so boats cannot surge independently. Protect rails, windows and hardware rather than placing one undersized fender at midship and hoping it stays in place.

Control people movement

Tell passengers where crossing is allowed and what they may hold. Step across only when boats are stable, use a handhold and avoid walking on upholstery, hatches or narrow gunwales. Children and pets need direct supervision because gaps change constantly.

Weight shifts can lower a fender, tighten a line or roll a small boat sharply. Keep coolers, fuel, grills and heavy gear from migrating randomly through the raft. Establish one primary social area instead of turning every deck into a walkway.

Treat swimming as a separate operation

Shut engines down and control keys before anyone enters the water. Account for every swimmer, designate a lookout and provide a defined entry and recovery point. Current can carry a swimmer under or away from the raft faster than people expect.

Keep propellers, outdrives, ladders and anchor lines visible in the plan. Do not restart any engine until swimmers are counted and physically aboard. Alcohol, sun and social distraction make a formal head count more important, not less.

Watch the combined system

Recheck anchor position, line chafe, fender height and weather through the day. Windage grows as boats are added, and wakes can load the outside boats heavily. A line that was comfortable at noon may become dangerous after a wind shift or falling water level.

For an overnight raft, assign checks and confirm bilge, battery, ventilation and carbon-monoxide precautions on every boat. Generators and engines can create exhaust exposure for neighboring cabins even when the operator's own cabin seems clear.

Separate before conditions force it

Agree on the departure order and which boat leaves first. Clear swimmers, collect loose gear and move passengers back to their own boats before lines begin coming off. Outside boats usually need the simplest escape path.

If weather, dragging or medical trouble develops, break the raft early while crews are organized. Social pressure is not a reason to consume the last safe departure window. A good raft-up ends with every boat able to maneuver independently.

Failure modes to avoid

  • Building the raft before the anchor boat is settled
  • Using too few fenders or no spring lines
  • Restarting an engine before every swimmer is aboard
  • Waiting too long to separate as weather changes

Field checklist

  • Location checked for depth, swing and wakes
  • Anchor boat and leader designated
  • Approach side, fenders and lines prepared
  • Bow, stern and spring control established
  • Passenger crossing point identified
  • Swimming lookout and head count assigned
  • Engines controlled before swimmers enter
  • Anchor position and chafe rechecked
  • Overnight exhaust and bilge risks reviewed
  • Breakaway order agreed before it is needed

Frequently asked questions

How many boats can safely raft together?

There is no universal number. The location, anchor system, boat sizes, weather, traffic, crew experience and local rules control the practical limit.

Should every boat deploy an anchor?

Not automatically. Multiple anchors can improve holding in some arrangements but can also cross, foul or complicate departure. Use a deliberate plan suited to the location.

Can a raft-up remain overnight?

Only when the anchoring, weather, watch plan, exhaust control, lights and local rules support it. An overnight raft requires more discipline than a daytime social stop.

What is the most useful line between boats?

Spring lines are essential because they limit fore-and-aft surging. Bow and stern lines alone often let boats saw against each other in wakes.

Verify current information

Equipment procedures, waterway rules and operating conditions can change. Use current manufacturer documentation, local notices and primary boating-safety sources for the exact boat and trip.
