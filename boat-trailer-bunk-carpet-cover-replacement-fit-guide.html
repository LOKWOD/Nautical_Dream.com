A torn carpet edge looks like a material problem, but the purchase decision is larger: the bunk board may be wet or split, the fasteners may be exposed, the brackets may be corroded, or the current support may not match the hull. Record the original system before removing anything, and treat a geometry change as trailer fit work—not a cosmetic repair.

Start with the boat-to-trailer support plan

Find the trailer manufacturer, model, serial or VIN, rated capacity and current parts drawing. Then identify the boat’s hull material, model, loaded weight, transom location, strakes, chines and any published trailer-support instructions. A covering cannot make a misplaced bunk safe.

ShoreLand’r’s fitting guidance illustrates why geometry is specific: its process checks transom alignment, keel and fender clearances, strake relationship and bunk or roller position. Those dimensions belong to that maker’s fitting system, not to every trailer. Photograph the loaded boat from the stern and both sides before removal, mark bracket locations reversibly, and measure only when the rig is secured on level ground.

Replace carpet when the support is sound and the maker still calls for carpet

Marine bunk carpet is a covering, not a repair for a rotten board. Inspect the full board after the boat is safely removed. Reject softness, splitting, severe fastener enlargement, permanent twist, crushed bracket zones or dimensions that do not match the approved part.

Load Rite describes its replacement carpet as marine grade, cut to its available bunk sizes and designed for minimal water retention. Mercury’s current replacement overview advises inspection before holes expose the hull and lists marine carpet and stainless staples among the supplies. Those statements support the category; the exact board, overlap, backing, staple size and attachment pattern still come from the trailer or material instructions.

Compare marine boat-trailer bunk carpet rolls by actual width, length, backing, water-retention claims and installation instructions. Buy from measured coverage, including the specified wrap and end treatment—not from trailer length alone.

Use a polymer cover only when hull and trailer guidance allow it

A smooth cover can reduce water retention and loading friction, but those are not universally desirable. Load Rite specifically recommends its Quick-Slide covers where bunks contact a metal hull to avoid trapping moisture in carpet fibers, while also telling buyers to check whether the boat manufacturer recommends the support system. That is maker-specific guidance, not permission to add a slick surface to every fiberglass, painted or aluminum hull.

Changing friction changes launch behavior. The boat must remain connected and controlled until the manufacturer’s launch sequence says otherwise; never release a winch line or safety connection early because the old carpet used to hold the boat. Reject exposed edges, fasteners in the contact zone, incompatible board profiles and any cover that lacks dimensions and exact attachment instructions.

Compare boat-trailer polymer bunk covers by board profile, hull-material guidance, fastener concealment and installation manual. Treat “fits 2×4” as a starting dimension, not full compatibility proof.

Choose a complete replacement bunk when the board or hidden hardware fails

A complete bunk can be the cleaner choice when the board is unsound, the carpet is bonded over damaged material or the fastener pattern cannot be reproduced safely. Match finished length, board section, bracket interface, bolt orientation, support angle and load path to the trailer parts list.

Do not assume a pre-carpeted board has the correct lumber, treatment, fasteners or bracket spacing for the boat. Pressure-treatment chemistry and metal compatibility can matter; use the trailer maker’s approved assembly or written specification. If the assembly requires drilling a new frame hole, welding, moving brakes or altering an aluminum or galvanized frame, stop for the maker or a qualified marine-trailer technician.

Compare complete carpeted boat-trailer bunk assemblies by finished dimensions, bracket geometry, fasteners and documented trailer compatibility. Skip a bundle that lists only board length.

What not to buy

Skip household carpet, indoor adhesive, exposed drywall or deck screws, untreated mystery lumber, loose plastic strips and listings whose only fit claim is “universal.” Skip a surface that covers cracks or corrosion before they can be inspected. Do not buy longer bunks to “add support” unless the manufacturer’s plan calls for them.

Also skip friction-changing covers when the crew has no controlled launch and retrieval procedure. A product that makes loading easier can make an inclined launch less forgiving. Keep an independent bow safety connection, use the winch and strap fit guide and follow the exact trailer instructions.

Unload and support the boat before removing a bunk

The safest job begins with the boat off the trailer and supported by a yard, marina or equipment and procedure intended for that hull. Never crawl under a boat supported only by a jack, winch, trailer tongue or improvised cribbing. Never loosen a bunk while someone is in the boat or while the rig is on a ramp.

If safe unloading is not practical, pay a qualified trailer or boatyard technician. This is especially appropriate for heavy boats, pontoons, damaged frames, unusual hulls, corroded hardware or any repair that changes load-bearing parts. The cost of controlled support is part of the project, not optional overhead.

Preserve the recorded geometry during installation

Transfer one measurement at a time from the approved drawing and pre-removal record. Orient brackets, carriage bolts, washers, lock hardware and staples exactly as instructed. Keep every sharp end away from the hull contact face, and preserve access for inspection and retightening.

Do not guess torque. Use the published value when one exists and a calibrated tool appropriate to the hardware. Replace corroded or distorted brackets with exact approved parts rather than hiding them beneath new carpet. Recheck keel, fender, frame, brake-line, wiring and transom clearances before the boat returns.

Prove the repair in three stages

  1. Empty trailer: verify symmetry, fastener concealment, drainage and bracket clearance.
  2. Controlled loading: load the boat with qualified help, confirm it lands on every intended support and photograph the settled geometry.
  3. Calm ramp: test launch and retrieval at a quiet familiar ramp with the bow controlled and no child or spotter between moving equipment.
  4. Road recheck: secure the boat independently, tow a short route, then inspect witness marks, hardware and hull contact.
  5. Scheduled inspection: follow the maker’s initial-use and seasonal intervals; replace covering before fasteners or board are exposed.

Stop for hard contact, uneven settling, new hull marks, bracket movement or a boat that tries to slide before the controlled release. Connect the result to the complete trailer safety checklist and launch checklist.

Know when the covering is not the problem

Crooked loading, hull distortion or repeat carpet damage can indicate wrong ramp depth, damaged brackets, bent frame, failed springs, misaligned axle, wrong tongue weight, inadequate capacity or a trailer that never fit the boat. New carpet will briefly hide those findings.

Use the guide-on fit guide only after the load-bearing supports are correct. If the boat or trailer maker cannot reconcile the hull with the current support pattern, place diagnosis and adjustment with an experienced dealer or marine-trailer shop.

Frequently asked questions

Can I put plastic bunk covers over worn carpet?

Only if the exact cover and trailer instructions allow that installation and the boat maker approves the contact surface. A cover must not hide failed carpet, board or hardware.

Should I replace just the carpet or the whole bunk?

Replace only the covering when the board, brackets, geometry and fasteners remain sound and approved. Use an exact complete assembly when the underlying structure fails.

Are slick bunk covers safe for every boat?

No. Hull material, loading method, ramp slope, support geometry and manufacturer guidance control. Reduced friction also changes launch behavior.
