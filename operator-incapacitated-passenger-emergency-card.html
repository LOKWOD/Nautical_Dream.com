If the Boat Operator Can’t Continue: Build a Passenger “Stop, Call, Position, Float” Card

Do not hand passengers a generic restart sequence. Pre-brief the exact boat, a conservative stop-and-call boundary, the radio, position source, flotation and who takes charge.

Editorial disclosure: Independent emergency-preparedness framework with no paid links. It cannot replace hands-on boating education, first-aid training, the exact boat and radio manuals, local law or emergency-dispatch instructions. Controls, shutdown, anchoring, towing, restarting and navigation are boat- and situation-specific. When life or the vessel is in grave and imminent danger, use the fastest available emergency route and follow the responsible authority.

If the only trained operator becomes ill, injured, unconscious or otherwise unable to continue, the passengers need fewer decisions—not a crash course at speed. A useful card says how this exact boat is brought to a safer immediate state, how to call, where to read position and how to keep people afloat while professional help responds. It does not turn an untrained passenger into a replacement skipper.

Choose the backup adult before departure

Name the adult most capable of listening, communicating and handling the exact boat. Show that person the throttle/shift, steering, engine stop, engine cut-off switch, radio, DSC distress control if equipped, GPS position display, life jackets, throwable device, fire extinguisher, first-aid kit, anchor only as appropriate, and the float plan. Do this tied securely at the dock with the engine and systems in a safe training state.

Do not assign a child the helm role or rely on a reluctant passenger. For a solo-adult family outing, reduce the route and risk accordingly. The crew briefing, passenger movement plan and accessible communication plan help make the briefing usable under stress.

Write only boat-specific immediate-control instructions

The card’s first box is not “drive home.” It is the exact, manual-consistent action needed to reduce an immediate collision, runaway-propeller or high-speed hazard. For many boats that may involve taking the throttle toward neutral while maintaining control, but control layouts, propulsion and conditions differ. A sailboat, jet drive, twin engine, electronic throttle and tiller are not interchangeable.

Write the sequence only after the owner’s manual and qualified instructor or technician confirm it. Photograph the actual control and label the card privately. Include what must not be touched. Never improvise a restart, engine cut-off bypass, fuel procedure or battery switch sequence. The Coast Guard explains that an engine cut-off link stops propulsion when the operator is displaced; it must be attached as federal rules and the exact system require.

Add the boat name, registration/call sign, description, home marina or launch, emergency contact, medical facts voluntarily provided for response, and where the float plan is filed. Laminate a copy at the helm without blocking labels or controls. Keep a phone copy offline. This four-box card is the page’s citation-ready authority asset.

Teach the VHF call before anyone needs it

The Coast Guard Navigation Center identifies VHF Channel 16 as the calling and distress-alerting channel. For grave and imminent danger, the voice distress call uses “MAYDAY” three times, identifies the vessel, gives position, nature of distress, help needed, number of people and other useful description, then waits for response. A DSC-equipped radio can send a digital alert, but position reaches rescuers only when the radio has internal GPS or a working GPS connection.

Show the backup adult how to power the exact radio, select 16, hold and release push-to-talk, speak slowly, read the coordinates and listen. Practice without transmitting a false distress call. Use the VHF distress-call drill and radio basics. For inland areas without Coast Guard VHF coverage, include 911 and local authority information while preserving the radio route when available.

Make position readable without navigation expertise

Choose one display the passenger can find. Set it to show latitude and longitude in a format the crew can read, and write the format on the card. Confirm the chartplotter, handheld GPS or phone works offline, has power and does not hide position behind several menus. The distress call should also include vessel name or description, direction of movement if known, people aboard and the operator’s condition.

Do not tell an untrained passenger to navigate through hazards or race to a marina. Their job is to report position and follow responder instructions while maintaining a safer immediate state. The offline navigation backup, float plan and navigation fundamentals strengthen the information without expanding the emergency card.

Put flotation on before the situation gets worse

The Coast Guard requires an approved wearable PFD for each person aboard and says wearables must be readily accessible; throwables must be immediately available. In this scenario, passengers should put on their fitted life jackets early when it is safe to do so. The backup adult accounts for every person and prevents children or distressed passengers from moving around the boat unnecessarily.

Do not jump into the water after an incapacitated operator unless trained rescue conditions and professional direction support it; a second victim makes the emergency worse. Do not use an inflatable PFD on a person for whom its label or condition is unsuitable. The life-jacket selection guide, children’s fit guide and water-entry boundaries should be resolved before departure.

Decide anchoring, drift and restart boundaries in advance

Anchoring can help in some locations and create danger in current, deep water, traffic, poor holding ground or near dams and underwater hazards. Restarting may restore steering or propulsion on one boat and create a propeller, fuel or runaway hazard on another. Towing introduces its own line-load and stability risks. None belongs on a universal card.

During the dockside review, ask a qualified instructor how the exact boat should be stabilized after immediate control is achieved and what a passenger should do while awaiting instructions. The card can say “do not anchor unless directed” or name a verified simple procedure when appropriate. The anchoring guide is education for trained operators, not an emergency shortcut.

Run a no-transmit, no-start dock drill

With the boat secured and systems made safe, give the backup adult the card. Ask them to point to the immediate-control item, power and select the radio as the manual permits without transmitting, locate coordinates, state the vessel description, find PFDs and the throwable, and read the float-plan contact. Time is less important than correctness and calm.

Then change one condition: the phone is locked, the plotter is dark, a child is crying, or the radio microphone is hard to reach. Fix the setup rather than blaming the passenger. Repeat at the start of the season and after helm, radio or phone changes. A qualified boating instructor can conduct an on-water familiarization at safe speed and conditions; the owner should not simulate incapacitation underway.

Use a conservative decision ladder during the real event

First address immediate collision and propeller danger using the verified boat-specific instruction. Second call for help by the fastest workable route. Third state position, boat description, people and medical problem. Fourth get flotation on and maintain a lookout. Follow the dispatcher or Coast Guard, and keep the radio channel available. Provide first aid only within training and dispatch guidance.

If the operator recovers, do not automatically resume the trip. A transient medical event, fall, head injury, confusion, chest pain or loss of consciousness needs professional evaluation. Preserve the incident timeline and any device data without delaying care. The boating-emergency guide and rescue-tool boundaries reinforce the same conservative posture.

Know what this card cannot solve

The card does not qualify a passenger to operate, does not replace state education requirements, does not guarantee radio coverage and does not make a medically unfit crew safe. It cannot account for every fire, grounding, flooding, person-overboard, severe-weather or collision scenario. Keep the trip inside the crew’s actual skills and file a Coast Guard-recommended float plan with a reliable person every time.

Skip the outing or add a trained operator when no adult can accept the backup role, the route exceeds reliable communications, weather reduces control margin, the only operator has an unstable condition, or the boat’s helm cannot be explained simply and safely. An instructor, physician, insurer, charter operator or other qualified professional may be needed. The smartest card sometimes says: do not leave the dock.

Frequently asked questions

Should an untrained passenger try to drive the boat back to the dock?

Not as a default. The passenger should reduce immediate danger using boat-specific pre-briefed actions, call for help, report position, use flotation and follow professional instructions.

When should a passenger say MAYDAY?

Use MAYDAY for grave and imminent danger requiring immediate assistance. Give the vessel identity, position, nature of distress, help needed and number of people, then listen.

Should the passenger restart the engine after using the cut-off switch?

Only if the exact boat procedure has been verified and the situation and responding authority support it. There is no safe universal restart instruction.
