A beacon is not an upgrade to a phone. It is a rescue path that must still work after the boat loses power, the operator enters the water or the crew becomes separated. NOAA distinguishes personal locator beacons from vessel-oriented EPIRBs, while a commercial satellite messenger uses a provider network and service terms. Start with the emergency architecture, not the screen, app or sale price.

Separate the three rescue paths

A PLB is a portable 406 MHz personal locator beacon. NOAA says it is carried by an individual and manually activated. An EPIRB is a maritime 406 MHz beacon designed for a vessel; Category I units use a float-free automatic-release arrangement when correctly installed, while Category II units are manually deployed. A satellite messenger is a commercial communications device whose SOS, messaging, coverage, account and response workflow depend on the exact model and provider.

Do not call all three “satellite communicators” and assume equivalent outcomes. A messenger may add valuable two-way text and routine check-ins. A registered 406 beacon is a dedicated alert to the international search-and-rescue satellite system. Some buyers reasonably carry both: one for dedicated distress alerting and one for two-way context. The marine communications guide shows where VHF, DSC, phone and satellite tools fit around them.

Choose person-centric or vessel-centric protection

A PLB makes sense when the protection must follow one person across a small boat, paddle craft, rental or shore excursion. That advantage exists only if it is actually secured to the person in a manufacturer-approved way and can be operated with cold, wet hands. A beacon inside a dry bag under a seat is boat cargo, not personal protection.

An EPIRB makes sense when the casualty model is the vessel itself and the beacon can be mounted where its release, flotation, antenna and access are not defeated. NOAA says a Category I bracket must be outside the cabin where the beacon can float free. Installation is not a generic screw pattern: follow the exact manual and use a qualified marine technician when structure, release path or wiring is uncertain. The passenger movement plan should include who retrieves a manually deployed unit.

Write a nine-line fit record before shopping

Record: rescue system; primary user; activation; GNSS location capability; flotation behavior; wear or mount location; rated operating duration; battery/service date; and registration or subscription owner. Copy each answer from current manufacturer documentation, not a marketplace bullet.

Add the actual route: open inland lake, coastal water, paddle craft, overnight cruise or offshore passage. Then write the casualty that matters—capsize, fire, sinking, person overboard or medical emergency. A fixed EPIRB does not follow a separated person. A pocket PLB does not automatically float free with a sinking boat. A messenger with an expired plan or empty battery cannot deliver the service implied by its SOS button. Reject any listing that hides the model manual, activation method, coded market or service terms.

Treat registration as part of commissioning

NOAA states that a new or used U.S.-coded 406 MHz PLB or EPIRB must be registered in the National Beacon Registration Database. Registration is free, must be updated when contact, vessel or ownership information changes, and is valid for two years before renewal. Confirm the 15-character beacon identifier against NOAA’s acknowledgment; do not trust a handwritten digit. A used beacon is not ready until the seller removes it from the old record and the buyer completes a new one.

Keep a protected copy of the confirmation, unique ID, model, serial, battery date and emergency contacts in the boat record system. A satellite messenger requires a separate provider account and current service status. Put the renewal owner and date on the same record. Registration does not replace a float plan, and a subscription receipt does not prove a test passed.

Reject the feature traps

Do not buy from color, app screenshots, “global” marketing, a generic SOS label or the promise that a device “floats.” Determine whether it floats in the operating orientation required for transmission, whether it needs a pouch, whether the antenna must be held clear of the water and whether it is approved for the market where it will be registered. Reject old 121.5/243 MHz-only EPIRBs; the Coast Guard states satellite processing for those frequencies ended in 2009.

Do not assume a PLB has two-way text, an EPIRB follows a person or a messenger enters the NOAA 406 system. Do not choose a Category I EPIRB if the boat cannot provide a compliant float-free location. Do not mount any emergency device where a cover, hardtop, hatch or loose gear can trap it. If the crew cannot explain the device in one sentence, the system is not commissioned.

Commission without creating a false alert

Photograph the device, label, mount and registration confirmation. Complete only the manufacturer’s self-test procedure. NOAA says self-test mode may be used as instructed; live transmission testing with an operational beacon requires formal coordination and must not be improvised. The Coast Guard likewise warns against radiating a live EPIRB signal outside approved test conditions. If the beacon accidentally activates, follow the label and official instructions immediately—do not simply turn it off and hope the alert disappears.

For a messenger, prove login, subscription, contacts, paired phone behavior and check-in delivery without pressing SOS. For a PLB, practice exposing the antenna and controls using an inert training discussion, never a live activation. For an EPIRB, inspect the bracket, release dates, lanyard and unobstructed path. A qualified dealer should service sealed batteries and hydrostatic releases as the exact manual requires.

Build the crew brief and carriage habit

Before departure, point to each device and say what it does: “VHF calls nearby boats and rescue stations,” “this 406 beacon alerts search and rescue,” and “this messenger sends routine updates through our provider.” Identify who carries the PLB, who can retrieve the EPIRB and who knows the messenger passcode. The VHF distress-call drill and DSC/MMSI setup remain separate layers.

At the ramp, verify device present, secured, in date and accessible. After the trip, dry and inspect it according to the manual, recharge only where applicable, and record any self-test result. Update NOAA after a vessel, owner, phone or emergency-contact change; update the commercial account whenever a contact or payment method changes. The equipment is ready only when the rescue path, record and human habit agree.

Frequently asked questions

Does a satellite messenger replace a PLB or EPIRB?

Not automatically. A commercial messenger uses its documented provider network and terms; a U.S.-coded 406 PLB or EPIRB uses the registered SARSAT rescue path. Compare the exact systems and casualty plan.

Do I need to register a 406 MHz beacon?

Yes. NOAA says U.S.-coded 406 MHz PLBs and EPIRBs must be registered, updated when information changes and renewed every two years.

Can I press the distress button to test it?

No. Use only the manufacturer’s self-test procedure. NOAA requires coordination for operational transmissions, and live distress activation can divert rescue resources.
