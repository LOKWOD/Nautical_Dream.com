Boat Air Draft and Bridge Clearance: Measure the Highest Fixed Point, Then Verify the Water

A charted number is not a promise that today’s boat fits. The boat changes with trim and equipment; the available opening changes with water level, span and bridge status.

Editorial disclosure: General U.S. navigation planning based on NOAA and U.S. Coast Guard sources checked October 23, 2026. This page is not a chart, water-level correction, bridge-opening instruction or guaranteed clearance. Use current official navigation products, notices, bridge regulations and local authority information for the actual route; when clearance is uncertain, stop in safe water and do not pass.

Air draft is the height from the waterline to the boat’s highest relevant point. For a trailerable family boat that point may be a folding antenna, anchor light, bimini frame, windshield, radar, mast, tower or temporarily raised equipment. Bridge clearance is published relative to a stated datum and sometimes a specific span or bridge position. Planning succeeds only when both records are current and the operator preserves room to stop, hold and turn before the structure.

Do not subtract the first number from the second until the bridge's datum, present water and intended span are understood through official sources. NOAA's Coast Pilot says bridge and cable clearances are referenced to specified water datums and must be reduced when water stands above the applicable reference. The exact adjustment belongs to the official product and waterbody—not a generic web calculator.

Measure the boat in every travel configuration

Create separate records for “everything deployed,” “normal underway,” and any manufacturer-approved lowered configuration. Measure from the actual waterline under a representative safe load, not from the trailer bunks or driveway. Identify the highest fixed object and every movable item: antenna, all-round light, bimini, radar, outriggers, wake tower, mast and electronics.

Use the boat and equipment manuals for folding, stowing and operating restrictions. Never lower a required navigation light or communication antenna while underway merely to fit. Never climb onto a top, tower or bow while drifting toward a bridge. A qualified marine technician should verify uncertain installations or recent modifications. Add the result to the vessel record.

Use the five-number bridge card

  1. Boat maximum: measured air draft in the exact route configuration, with date and load note.
  2. Bridge identity: waterway, mile/position, name and intended navigable span.
  3. Published clearance: value, open/closed condition and the stated datum from the current official source.
  4. Current water/status: official tide, river/lake stage, bridge notice, construction or restriction source and time checked.
  5. Operator decision: approved opening request, documented safe alternative, or no-go—plus upstream and downstream holding points.

This card is a source-and-decision record, not a clearance certificate. Leave the available-clearance calculation blank unless the responsible official source supplies the correct method and inputs for that bridge. Never fill the gap with a crowd-sourced app screenshot.

Read the NOAA product beyond the large number

NOAA electronic navigational charts are official vector data used by many recreational chartplotters. The Coast Pilot adds text about bridge positions, clearances, cables and local cautions. Use the current ENC, applicable Coast Pilot, navigation updates and Local Notice to Mariners for the route. Confirm that the chartplotter's data subscription and display actually expose the needed object attributes.

A symbol or label can refer to one span, a closed draw position or a charting datum that differs from the water-level display in front of you. NOAA notes that horizontal clearances may appear in chart data even when not repeated in Coast Pilot text. Keep the chartplotter selection and navigation-rules review as separate layers; neither replaces source interpretation.

Treat drawbridge operation as a schedule, not a favor

Federal drawbridge rules in 33 CFR Part 117 vary by waterway and bridge. Some open on signal, some on fixed intervals, some require advance notice, and some restrict recreational openings during named periods. Find the section for the actual bridge and check current notices. The number posted at a bridge may be useful but is not a substitute for advance planning where notice is required.

Prepare the correct VHF or sound-signal method and plain request before entering the approach. Do not circle in a channel, block traffic or drift into a closed span while negotiating. If the bridge does not respond or cannot open, move to the preselected safe hold or turn point. The VHF basics and sound-signal guide support communication without inventing a universal bridge call.

Set the hold point before the bridge appears

On the route plan, mark the last location where the boat can slow, hold or turn without entering restricted water, shoal, current, traffic or another hazard. Then mark a second fallback farther away. The approach should never depend on stopping under the span or reversing into following traffic.

Account for wind, current, wake, steering at low speed and the boat's stopping distance. Keep passengers seated and the lookout assigned. If an operator must leave the helm to lower approved equipment, do that secured at a safe dock or anchorage under the manual—not underway in the approach.

Read clearance gauges as evidence, not permission

A bridge gauge can help show the vertical opening at its location, but the intended span, units, mounting, water motion, debris and viewing angle matter. Do not transfer a gauge reading from one edge of the structure to another opening. If it is missing, damaged, obscured, ambiguous or conflicts with official information, treat clearance as unresolved.

Slow enough to interpret the approach without losing control, but do not creep into a point of no return. A wake can raise the boat and a wave can lower the opening at the wrong moment. A moving antenna is not a sacrificial feeler. Contact with a bridge, cable or structure can injure people and damage both boat and public infrastructure.

Build a configuration-change log

Re-measure after adding a bimini, enclosure, radar, antenna, anchor light, roof rack, tower, hardtop equipment or new loading pattern. Record who measured, where the waterline was, which equipment was deployed and what assumptions remain. Photograph the highest point and its approved stowed position without treating the photo as a scale drawing.

Do the same before a borrowed, chartered or recently purchased boat takes a bridge route. A sales specification may list a nominal height that excludes options, antennas or the boat's loaded waterline. Use the used-boat verification discipline and operator handoff card to keep the limit visible.

Use a go, hold, reroute decision—not “probably”

Go only when boat configuration, official bridge identity, datum, current water, opening rule and status are all resolved with a conservative operating decision. Hold at the planned safe point while contacting the bridge or authority. Reroute when the route offers a documented alternative. No-go when any material input conflicts, is stale or cannot be verified.

Do not invent a universal clearance margin. Authorities, waterbodies, structures and vessel motion differ. A number that seems generous on flat water can be consumed by an incorrect datum, rising stage, wake or equipment left up. The safe choice is not an arithmetic contest; it is preserving a route that does not require guessing.

Practice the worksheet without approaching a bridge

Use a known boat at the dock. Point to the waterline, highest fixed point, every movable item and the approved stow method. Complete a sample bridge card from current official sources. Then identify where the real route would allow a safe hold and turn. Stop before anyone starts treating the exercise as permission to attempt the passage.

The low-energy version is a five-minute equipment walk-around and source check. The family role is simple: one operator navigates and communicates, one adult manages passengers and the lookout, and nobody changes the boat's configuration without the operator's instruction. If the plan cannot be explained calmly ashore, it is not ready for the approach.

Frequently asked questions

What is boat air draft?

It is the vertical height from the waterline to the boat’s highest relevant point in the configuration being used. Load, trim and movable equipment can change it.

Can I use the bridge-clearance number shown in a chartplotter?

Use current official chart data and applicable Coast Pilot information, but also identify the datum, span, bridge condition and current water level. A displayed number alone is not a guarantee.

How much clearance margin should I allow?

There is no safe universal number in this guide. The responsible authority, structure, water level, vessel motion and exact boat configuration determine the decision; when uncertain, do not pass.
