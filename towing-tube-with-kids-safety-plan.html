Towing a Tube With Kids: Spotter, Signals, Shutdown, and the No-Go Gate

The fun part starts after the operator, observer and rider agree on the stop—and after the water leaves room for it.

By Nautical Dream Editorial DeskUpdated September 12, 202616 minute read

Tube towing turns three people into one operating system: the driver controls the boat, the observer watches the rider and the rider communicates through distance, spray and noise. New York Navigation Law §73 makes key boundaries explicit: a separate observer at least ten years old, no towing from sunset to sunrise and a securely fastened U.S. Coast Guard-approved personal flotation device for the person being towed. Those are legal floors. A family plan should create more margin than the minimum.

Know the law before the rope comes aboard

New York’s current statute defines a towing device broadly enough to include a tube, tow rope, water skis, surfboard, aquaplane and related device. The operator must have another person aboard, at least ten years old, positioned to observe the person being towed. Towing is prohibited from sunset to sunrise, and the rider must wear a securely fastened Coast Guard-approved PFD.

The law also prohibits operating or manipulating the boat or device so the rider strikes an object or person, and it bars reckless or negligent towing. Local waterbody rules can add limits. Check the current state law and local regulation every season and whenever boating somewhere new.

Use a real observer, not a mirror and not a distracted child

The driver looks ahead, controls speed and reads traffic. The observer faces the rider, watches the rope and tube and communicates continuously. In New York, that observer is legally separate from the operator and at least ten; for a family crew, choose someone mature enough to recognize distress, relay signals accurately and stay on task.

A phone, camera or conversation defeats the job. The observer should have a secure seated position, clear aft view and no need to lean outside the boat. If the observer cannot see the rider through spray, glare, trim or passenger placement, do not start.

Fit the rider’s life jacket for this body and activity

Use a properly fitted, securely fastened, Coast Guard-approved life jacket suited to the rider’s weight and the activity. Check the label rather than color or brand. Tighten it so it cannot ride over the chin or ears, then test mobility and breathing before boarding.

An inflatable belt or adult-size jacket is not a shortcut for a child. New York separately requires all children under twelve aboard a vessel under 65 feet to wear a Coast Guard-approved life jacket unless in a fully enclosed cabin. The life-jacket guide explains fit and label checks without pretending one model fits every body.

Match tube, rope and tow point to the boat

Read the tube, rope, tow-point and boat manuals together. Respect rider count, combined weight, inflation range, rope strength, connector method and boat-capacity limits. Attach only to the manufacturer-designated tow point. A cleat, rail, tower, ski eye or transom fitting is suitable only when its maker explicitly rates it for that tow load.

Inspect seams, handles, valves, cover, rope, splices, connectors and attachment hardware before every session. Reject cuts, abrasion, sun damage, knots, corrosion or uncertain ratings. Keep the rope outside the boat and clear of the propeller before starting.

Build a no-go gate before enthusiasm votes

Do not tow in lightning risk, fog, darkness, strong wind, poor visibility, heavy traffic, cold exposure, floating debris or water too confined for a wide controlled turn and protected pickup. Add a lower cutoff for a new rider, unfamiliar boat or inexperienced observer.

The driver makes the final decision and does not negotiate it from the helm. Use the family weather plan and the predeparture checklist. If the water is marginal, swim from shore where permitted, run a slow sightseeing loop or use the land-day backup.

Brief four signals and one automatic stop

Agree on simple hand signals before the rider enters the water: speed up, slow down, stop and rider okay. Demonstrate them. The observer repeats the rider’s signal to the driver and confirms the driver saw it. A dropped hand, unusual stillness, repeated fall or any uncertain signal means stop.

Create one automatic rule: if communication fails, the tow ends. Shouting over the engine is not a protocol. Keep the first run short enough that the child learns the signals before fatigue and spray make them harder.

Enter the water only after the propulsion boundary is controlled

Choose open water away from the ramp, docks, swimmers, anchored boats, shore, markers, shallow hazards and traffic lanes. Stop the boat. Follow the engine maker’s shutdown and ignition-control procedure before anyone approaches the stern or rope. Account for every person and the rope.

Hand the tube and rope out deliberately; do not throw a coil across people. The rider settles on the tube with the PFD fastened and hands on designed handles. Take slack out at idle only after the observer confirms a clear line and the rider gives the ready signal.

Accelerate for control, not surprise

Use the tube and boat maker’s guidance, the rider’s age and experience, water conditions and local limits. There is no universal safe speed. Begin gently, keep the rider inside a wide obstacle-free envelope and avoid abrupt throttle, tight turns and wake-chasing.

The tube travels on a different path from the boat in a turn and can swing much closer to shore, docks or other vessels. Plan for the outside arc before steering. Do not cross another boat’s wake to manufacture airtime. The wake-crossing guide applies to the people aboard, while a towed rider adds a separate uncontrolled path.

The observer reports continuously

Use short calls: “rider okay,” “slow,” “down,” “rope clear” or “stop.” The observer watches grip, posture, PFD position, facial expression, cold stress, rope location and approaching wakes. A smiling rider can still be losing grip or becoming too tired to climb aboard.

Stop before the child asks if posture degrades or attention fades. Schedule short rounds with full rest, water and warmth between them. Rotate riders only when the complete setup and capacity limits remain valid.

A fall starts a protected pickup, not a fast circle

Reduce speed promptly while maintaining control. Keep the rider in sight, alert traffic and approach slowly from the safe side dictated by wind, waves and the boat’s handling. Never back toward the rider. Keep the rope away from the propeller and do not let the hull drift over it.

Before the rider reaches the stern or boarding ladder, secure propulsion against starting according to the boat and engine procedure. The observer maintains visual contact throughout. If the rider may be injured, cold or entangled, call for help and use the practiced recovery plan rather than pulling impulsively.

Stop for more than an obvious injury

End the session for pain, head impact, breathing trouble, unusual confusion, weakness, vomiting, persistent cough after immersion, uncontrolled shivering or inability to reboard normally. Seek emergency care for serious symptoms. Do not use another run as a medical test.

Inspect the PFD, tube, rope and tow point after a hard fall or shock load. Record damage and retire questionable gear. A qualified marine technician should inspect a tow point, tower or structural attachment that moved, cracked, deformed or made an abnormal sound.

Who should skip tubing today

  • A crew without a separate legal and attentive observer.
  • A rider whose PFD fit, swimming comfort or consent is uncertain.
  • A boat without a documented rated tow point and compatible equipment.
  • Water without a wide obstacle-free operating and pickup area.
  • A driver pressured to produce speed, sharp turns or airtime.

“Not today” protects future days. Tow sports are optional; the boat’s safety margin is not.

The 90-second tubing brief

  1. Confirm current state and local towing rules.
  2. Assign driver and dedicated observer.
  3. Fit and fasten the rider’s approved PFD.
  4. Inspect tube, inflation, rope, connectors and rated tow point.
  5. Set the weather, traffic and obstacle no-go gate.
  6. Practice speed-up, slow-down, stop and okay signals.
  7. Explain the fall and engine-secured pickup.
  8. Run one short gentle pass, then reassess.

Frequently asked questions

Does New York require a spotter when towing a tube?

Yes. Navigation Law §73 requires a person other than the operator, at least ten years old, positioned to observe the person being towed.

Can children ride a tube without a life jacket?

No in New York. A person being towed must wear a securely fastened U.S. Coast Guard-approved personal flotation device.

What is a safe tubing speed for a child?

There is no universal number. Use the equipment manuals, local rules, rider ability, boat handling and conditions; begin gently and preserve a wide clear envelope.

Can I tow after sunset?

New York prohibits towing a person from sunset to sunrise, apart from a narrow professional-performance exception.

How should I pick up a fallen rider?

Keep the rider in sight, slow promptly, approach under control without backing toward them, keep the rope clear and secure propulsion before they approach the stern.
