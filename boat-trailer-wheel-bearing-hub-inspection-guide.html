Boat-trailer wheel bearings live in a difficult combination of highway speed, load, heat, water and long idle periods. The useful plan is not ‘add grease before every trip.’ It is a traceable system: identify the axle and hub, follow that maker's service method, establish a cool-running baseline, stop for meaningful changes and carry only a spare whose dimensions and hardware have been confirmed.

This identity card is the citation-ready asset. A shop invoice, axle tag photo and manual page are better evidence than a marketplace fit list. Keep a paper copy in the tow vehicle and a digital copy with the trailer record.

Treat every maintenance interval as manufacturer-specific

Dexter's current complete service manual says proper lubrication is essential and gives a 12-month or 12,000-mile interval for the grease-lubricated bearings covered by that manual. That is useful primary guidance for those Dexter assemblies, not a universal interval for every boat trailer. Other axle makers and proprietary lubrication systems can specify different parts, greases, procedures and inspection points.

Use the schedule for the exact axle and account for immersion, mileage, storage, brake type and service history. A used trailer with missing records should not inherit an optimistic date. Have a qualified trailer technician open, inspect and establish the baseline if the identity, seal condition, bearing adjustment or brake contamination risk is uncertain.

Do not turn a grease fitting into a diagnosis

A spindle fitting can be part of a designed lubrication system, but the fitting alone does not prove which procedure applies. Dexter's E-Z Lube instructions, for example, call for a manual grease gun, hub rotation and slow pumping; the company warns against pneumatic guns because they can force grease past a seal. That direction belongs to that system.

Do not pump grease until resistance ‘feels right,’ mix an unknown grease with a new tube or assume added grease repairs a damaged seal, rough race, incorrect adjustment or brake contamination. Dexter explicitly cautions against mixing several thickener families and directs removal of old grease when changing types. Follow the exact manual or stop.

Separate inspection, lubrication and replacement

An exterior check looks for a displaced cap, lubricant sling, rust tracks, looseness, wheel damage and brake-fluid or grease evidence. Scheduled service may require disassembly, cleaning, race and roller inspection, seal replacement, correct packing, adjustment and wheel-nut torque. Replacement adds part selection and verification. These are different jobs.

If the wheel has detectable play beyond the maker's procedure, roughness, noise, a loose cap, leaking seal, blue or scored parts, contaminated brakes or a repeat heat anomaly, do not solve it at the ramp with more grease. Tow or service the trailer as appropriate. Bearing adjustment and brake work can create wheel-separation or stopping risks; use a qualified technician if the exact procedure is not routine for you.

Use temperature as a comparison, not a magic number

At a safe pull-off, keep the rig coupled, set the tow vehicle as its manual directs and stay out of traffic. Without crawling under the trailer, aim an infrared thermometer at the same exposed area of each comparable hub. Record ambient conditions, elapsed drive time, speed pattern, braking and reading location. Tandem-axle and braked hubs should be compared with truly similar positions.

A single temperature is not a pass/fail standard. Sun, braking, bearing preload, load, road grade and measurement surface all matter, and an infrared tool reads surfaces rather than the bearing itself. The useful signal is a repeatable departure from that trailer's established pattern or a rapid change accompanied by odor, noise, smoke, leakage or wheel behavior. Those symptoms mean stop and arrange qualified help.

Make the first stop early enough to matter

After service, wheel removal or a long storage period, plan an early safe stop before committing to an isolated highway. Walk the rig, compare hubs, inspect tires and straps, and look for loosened hardware or lubricant evidence. Repeat after sustained speed or heavy braking when the route makes that useful.

Never touch a suspicious hub with a bare hand as a test, and never enter a traffic lane to get a reading. If no safe inspection place exists, keep driving only as conditions safely permit and choose the next proper stop; a roadside ritual is not worth a struck-by hazard.

Reject the almost-right spare

A spare hub is a roadside recovery option only when it is the correct assembly and the operator can install it safely. Count studs and measure the actual bolt circle correctly, but do not stop there. Hub capacity, inner and outer bearing IDs, races, seal dimensions, spindle profile, wheel pilot, lug hardware and brake configuration must all match.

Inventory the dust cap, cotter pin or retaining hardware, seal, bearings, races and the exact socket and torque tool required by the maker. Keep lubricated parts sealed from dirt and water. A loose box of ‘common’ bearings is not a complete recovery system.

Pre-fit the spare under controlled conditions

Verification belongs in a shop or driveway where the trailer can be supported exactly as its manufacturer requires—not on a hot shoulder. A qualified technician can confirm the hub assembly, bearing seating, seal and adjustment and can document wheel-nut torque. Repack or preserve the spare afterward according to the maker's directions.

Do not learn jack placement from social media. Trailer frames, axles and suspension parts are not interchangeable lifting points, and a boat trailer can shift. Never work beneath an inadequately supported trailer. If safe field replacement is outside the crew's training, carry the exact parts for the service provider and a roadside-assistance plan.

Protect brake assemblies from grease

Grease pushed past a seal can reach brake components. That is not cosmetic. Braking performance and fire risk are safety concerns, and contaminated friction material requires assessment under the brake manufacturer's instructions. Do not spray solvents blindly or continue towing because the wheel still turns.

For surge, electric drum or electric-over-hydraulic systems, identify the brake maker as well as the axle maker. The boat-trailer brake-system guide explains why actuator, controller, brakes and legal capacity form one system. A hub service that ignores the brakes is incomplete.

Log evidence after retrieval and storage

Record mileage, immersion, service date, parts used, lubricant, seal, adjustment method, technician and torque specification source. After retrieval, inspect for a displaced cap or fresh sling. After storage, rotate and inspect only as the maker directs; surface rust, condensation and flat-spotted tires can complicate the first tow.

Pair the record with the trailer inflator system and trailer safety checklist. Tires, hubs, brakes, lights, coupling and load are one road system. A cool hub does not excuse an overloaded or poorly secured trailer.

Know the stop-now signals

Stop in the safest available location for smoke, grinding, a wobbling wheel, a missing or displaced cap, lubricant on the wheel, a strong hot-brake odor, visible brake-fluid leak or a hub that changes sharply against its paired baseline. Keep people away from traffic and hot components. Do not pour water on a hot hub or immerse it to cool it.

Call emergency services for fire or immediate roadway danger; otherwise use a qualified trailer service or towing provider. Continuing ‘just to the ramp’ can turn a serviceable assembly into a lost wheel, damaged spindle or collision.

Who should not do roadside hub work

Skip roadside replacement if the stop is exposed to traffic, the shoulder is soft or sloped, weather reduces visibility, the rig cannot be secured, the correct lifting and support equipment is absent, brakes are involved beyond your competence or part identity is uncertain. A spare does not create a safe workspace.

Owners with oil-bath hubs, unusual spindles, integrated brake drums, repeated seal failures or unknown prior repairs should establish a professional plan before travel. The best roadside bearing job is often the one prevented by documented service and conservative stops.

Close with a five-line departure record

  1. Identity: axle, hub, bearings, seal and brake system confirmed.
  2. Service: date, method, lubricant and technician recorded.
  3. Baseline: comparable hub readings recorded after a known-good tow.
  4. Recovery: exact spare, tools and assistance plan verified.
  5. Boundary: stop-now symptoms and safe pull-offs named.

That record is more useful than a toolbox full of uncertain parts. Review it before the pre-departure check, and update it after any wheel, hub, brake or axle work.

Frequently asked questions

How hot is too hot for a boat-trailer hub?

There is no safe universal surface-temperature number in this guide. Compare like hubs under like conditions and follow the axle, bearing and brake manufacturers. A sharp change, major side-to-side difference or heat plus odor, noise, smoke or leakage is a stop-and-service signal.

Can I add grease before every launch?

Only if the exact axle or hub manufacturer's procedure calls for it. Overfilling, mixing incompatible grease or using the wrong gun and method can damage seals or contaminate brakes.

Is a pre-greased hub ready to install?

Not automatically. Confirm every dimension, capacity, seal, bearing, race, lubricant and hardware detail, and verify the assembly under controlled conditions before treating it as a spare.

Should I touch the hub to check heat?

Use a repeatable noncontact method from a safe location. A bare-hand touch can cause a burn and still does not diagnose the internal bearing.
