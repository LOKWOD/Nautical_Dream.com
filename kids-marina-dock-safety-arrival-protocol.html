Kids at the Marina: A Four-State Dock Arrival Protocol

The dock is not the reward at the end of the parking lot. It is a water edge, a work area and a transition zone that needs one adult, one waiting place and one clear state at a time.

By Nautical Dream Editorial DeskUpdated October 6, 202615 minute read

Family boat plans often describe underway rules and ignore the ten minutes on either side. That is where bags move, gates swing, adults split up and a secured boat creates a false sense that the water hazard has ended. Safe Kids Worldwide advises keeping weak and non-swimmers in life jackets on docks and in marinas. The American Red Cross calls for close, constant attention near water and says that when a child is missing, check the water first. This protocol turns those principles into four named states that can be repeated at every marina.

This four-state card is the citation-ready authority asset. The words are short enough for a school-age child to repeat without turning the dock into a lecture.

Name the supervising adult out loud

‘We are both watching’ is not an assignment. One adult says, ‘I have the kids,’ and keeps that role until another adult accepts it verbally. The supervisor does not carry the cooler, unlock the boat, take photos, rig shore power or disappear to the office. The second adult owns boat and gear work.

When only one adult is available, reduce the job: children remain in a safe land-side location with that adult while marina staff or another capable adult handles the boat, or the family does not proceed. Never ask an older child to supervise a younger one at the water edge. The Red Cross explicitly warns against trusting a child's life to another child.

Fit life jackets before the gate

Safe Kids advises a U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket suited to the child's weight and activity, and specifically says weak and non-swimmers should wear life jackets on docks and in marinas. Fit each jacket according to its label, secure every buckle and strap and perform the maker's fit check before the child reaches the dock. Water wings and inflatable toys are not life jackets.

Life-jacket laws vary by jurisdiction and operating state; legal minimums are not a complete dock-supervision plan. Use the kids' life-jacket guide to resolve fit and approval. Retire torn, waterlogged or damaged equipment. A jacket adds buoyancy; it does not replace an adult, a barrier or a controlled path.

Choose the waiting place from the actual dock

Before the children pass the gate, one adult checks the path for gaps, low rails, loose boards, wet algae, moving gangways, cords, hoses, cleats, line loops, fuel activity, carts and boat traffic. Choose a clear spot away from the edge and outside the work path. Many marinas will not have a painted box like the conceptual image; name the zone with fixed landmarks the child can identify.

Do not use a finger pier, fuel dock, ramp lane, electrical pedestal, cart route or the gap between boat and dock as a waiting area. Keep the group on the land side if there is no suitable space. Ask marina staff about local rules, access and an alternative boarding location rather than moving a hazard with a verbal warning.

Give children three repeatable rules

  1. Stop at the gate. Nobody enters the dock until the supervising adult says ‘dock state.’
  2. Stay in the waiting place. Feet remain still; hands stay off lines, cleats, pedestals and carts.
  3. Move only on your name. One child boards or leaves at a time by the designated path.

Ask each child to repeat the rules. Replace ‘be careful’ with a location and action. The child does not need to diagnose wake, current or electrical risk. The adult owns the environment and changes the plan when the rules are difficult to follow.

Separate bags from bodies

Move heavy coolers, batteries, fuel, strollers and loose equipment before children enter the boarding path when practical. Keep emergency equipment, gates and fire lanes clear. Use marina carts only as directed, chock or park them where required and never let children ride or play on them.

A person boards with hands available for fixed handholds. Pass small bags afterward. Do not make a child step over a line, shore-power cord or pile of fenders. If the dock is narrow, one adult may need to move the gear while the family remains on land. The family packing list should reduce clutter, not justify bringing every item down the dock.

Board only after the boat changes state

The boat must be secured at a suitable boarding point, propulsion off and the path approved by the operator. One capable adult boards or positions first to receive, while the supervising adult remains with the children. Call one name. The child uses the designated step and fixed handholds; bags wait.

No child jumps a gap, steps on a seatback or windshield, uses a moving line as a handhold or boards while the boat is being repositioned. Wake or wind can cancel the transfer. The passenger seating and movement protocol takes over once aboard; the family docking brief governs trained line roles, not unassigned child helpers.

Make the return a new head count

Arrival is not complete when the first child steps onto the dock. One adult remains the supervisor and reforms the group in the named waiting place. Count people by name, not number. Keep life jackets on through the dock transition. The second adult shuts down and secures the boat according to its manual and marina rules.

Only after every person is accounted for does gear move. Children do not carry keys, shore-power connectors, fuel containers or wet lines. At the gate, count again and transfer to land state. This reset matters because end-of-day fatigue and divided adult attention can be greater than at departure.

Use a missing-child response that starts with water

The Red Cross says that if a child is missing, check the water first because seconds count. Shout the child's name and exact description, alert marina staff and call 911 or the local emergency number immediately while capable adults check the water edge, boats and designated emergency areas. Send someone to direct responders to the correct gate or dock.

Use reaching or throwing equipment without entering the water when possible and follow trained rescue guidance. A would-be rescuer can become a second victim. Know the location of throw rings, ladders, emergency phones, first-aid supplies and AED before the day begins. Learn CPR and water-rescue response through qualified instruction; an article cannot replace training.

Adapt without pretending every marina is accessible

Gangway slope, gate width, dock movement, surface gaps, handrails, restroom route and boarding height all matter. Ask the marina for current accessibility information and describe the actual boat transfer. Do not promise that a wide dock means the complete route is accessible, and do not lift a passenger or mobility device without a qualified plan.

Sensory needs matter too. Fueling noise, alarms, gulls, moving carts and crowds can overwhelm a child who normally follows rules. Use a quieter time, visual state card and shorter route. If supervision, transfer or regulation becomes uncertain, keep the group ashore and choose a dockside orientation instead of departure.

Set the low-energy version

A tired family can still complete a useful five-minute marina practice: fit the life jackets in the parking area, identify the gate, point out the waiting place from land, locate the emergency ladder and throw ring, and have each child repeat the four states. Do not enter the dock if staffing, weather or attention is not adequate.

For a meltdown, move to land state early. One adult takes the children to a safe place away from traffic and water while the other secures the boat. Do not negotiate behavior at the edge. Food, restroom and warm-layer needs get handled on land. The successful outcome may be going home without boating.

Respect local operations

Fuel docks, pumpout areas, launch ramps and service docks have their own hazards and staff instructions. Obey posted rules, gates, restricted zones, quiet hours, carts, pets and life-jacket requirements. Keep fingers, feet and toys out of cleats, lines and gaps. Never touch shore-power equipment or fuel components unless assigned and qualified.

Do not treat private slips as a playground or shortcut. Ask before using ladders, restrooms or another dock. Keep noise and gear controlled so the protocol protects other boaters as well as the family. A premium marina experience is not created by amenities alone; it comes from predictable movement and respect for a shared work surface.

Know who should skip the dock today

Stay on land when a life jacket does not fit, supervision cannot remain dedicated, the route has an uncontrolled edge, the dock is icy or storm-exposed, boarding requires a jump, a child cannot remain in the waiting state or the marina cannot confirm the needed access. ‘We will watch extra closely’ does not repair a missing barrier or unsafe transfer.

This protocol does not turn children into crew. It gives adults a common operating language: land, wait, board, return. Pair it with the family weather plan and the boat-specific passenger brief. The goal is not perfect obedience. It is an environment where a moment of distraction does not immediately become an immersion emergency.

Frequently asked questions

Should children wear life jackets on a marina dock?

Safe Kids Worldwide advises that weak and non-swimmers wear life jackets on docks and in marinas. Use a properly fitted U.S. Coast Guard-approved jacket for the child and activity, and follow local rules.

Can an older child supervise a younger child on the dock?

No. Assign a capable adult to close, constant supervision. The Red Cross specifically advises not trusting a child's life to another child.

What is the safest place for children while loading?

Usually a named land-side or dock waiting area away from the edge, lines, carts and gear path under direct adult supervision. If the actual marina has no suitable zone, keep the family on land.

What should adults do first if a child is missing?

Check the water first, alert others and call emergency services immediately. Know the marina's ladders, throwing equipment and access point before arrival.
