After the Storm: Inspect the Dock, the Boat and the First Start in That Order

The storm ending does not make the scene safe. Start from land, treat utilities and structure as unknown, document before moving evidence and earn the right to energize or start anything.

By Nautical Dream Editorial DeskUpdated October 7, 202617 minute read

A post-storm inspection is not a scavenger hunt for cosmetic damage. It is a sequence that protects people first, keeps evidence intact and prevents the first switch or engine start from turning hidden water, fuel or electrical damage into a larger loss. This protocol is intentionally conservative. Boat manuals, marina closures, emergency officials, utilities and qualified technicians outrank a generic checklist.

RedStay off: energized or unstable dock, fuel odor, fire, sinking, active leak or official closure
YellowDo not start: uncertain structure, water intrusion, damaged wiring, propulsion strike or contaminated fuel
GreenProceed only after documented checks and boat-specific instructions agree
EscalateMarina, emergency services, insurer, salvage contractor or qualified marine technician as the scene requires

Use the red-yellow-green no-go card

StateObserved conditionAction
RedDowned power, arcing, fuel sheen or strong odor, fire, unstable dock, sinking boat, active flooding, blocked emergency access or official closureStay away, warn others and call the appropriate emergency, marina, utility or pollution authority
YellowUnknown shore power, distorted structure, water above normal bilge level, damaged battery/wiring, grounding or impact evidence, uncertain fuel or control systemDo not energize, start or move; document and obtain qualified direction
GreenAccess authorized; structure, utilities and vessel checks show no disqualifying condition; manual procedures are completeProceed deliberately to a controlled systems check with an abort point

This card is triage, not certification. One red condition stops the visit. “Green” means no stop condition was found at this level; it never proves the boat seaworthy.

Wait for access authorization

Check official weather alerts, marina messages, harbor or park closures and utility instructions before traveling. A clear sky does not cancel lightning, flooding, unstable trees, submerged roads or emergency operations. Never bypass a gate, closure tape or staff direction to check property. Tell someone where you are going and do not inspect alone when conditions justify a second capable adult.

Wear a properly fitted life jacket near storm-elevated water and dress for footing, temperature and debris. Bring a charged phone, flashlight, camera, notebook and only the personal protective equipment you are trained to use. Do not bring children or pets into a damage scene. If emergency crews, utility workers or salvage teams are present, stay clear and follow their direction.

Approach from dry land and stop early

From a safe public or authorized point, photograph the entire scene before stepping onto any dock. Look for shifted pilings, missing decking, tilted ramps, floating debris, trapped boats, pulled cleats, stretched utilities, displaced fuel equipment, leaning trees and changing water. Compare the waterline and boat position with normal reference points.

Assume wet surfaces, mud, algae and silt are slippery. Never step onto a dock that is detached, twisted, submerged, moving abnormally or loaded by debris. Do not walk under damaged roofs, trees or suspended equipment. If safe access is uncertain, stop and send the overview photos to marina management, the insurer or a qualified marine contractor. Property can wait; a compromised structure does not owe the owner a warning.

Treat dock electricity as unknown

Water and damaged shore-power equipment are a potentially lethal combination. Do not touch a pedestal, cord, metal dock component, boat rail or water surface when electrical status is uncertain. Do not reset a breaker, unplug a wet connector or use a household tester to improvise clearance. Keep others out and contact the marina or utility emergency route.

Even when power appears off, damaged conductors or automatic restoration can change the condition. A qualified marina electrician or marine technician should inspect affected shore-power and vessel systems using applicable standards and manufacturer procedures. The shore-power pedestal guide explains the normal boundaries; storm damage makes those boundaries stricter. Electrical odor, heat, discoloration, arcing or tingling is a red stop.

Check dock structure before boat hardware

Only after authorized safe access, inspect the path underfoot: gangway attachment, decking, rails, hinges, float alignment, pilings, cleats and ladders. Look for fasteners pulled proud, gaps, uplift, cracked welds and hidden load from debris. Do not remove large debris or release a loaded line until a competent person understands what it is holding.

A cleat that looks intact may have moved below the deck. A floating dock can hide damaged connections under water. Mark the area, photograph it and report it. Do not use the boat as a handrail or transfer platform across a damaged section. If the dock cannot safely carry the inspection, the boat requires marina, salvage or contractor access—not a longer step.

Read lines and fenders as load records

Photograph every line before adjustment. Note chafe, melted fibers, glazing, pulled splices, bent hardware, unusual tension and the direction the boat traveled. Check fenders for displacement and hard compression. Those details can explain contact and help an insurer or repairer reconstruct the event.

Do not stand inside a bight or in the recoil path of a loaded line. Do not cut a line under strain unless emergency professionals direct the action and the consequences are controlled. Add or adjust lines only from a safe position with appropriate skill. The dock-lines guide is for normal mooring; storm-loaded gear may need a marina or salvage crew.

Inspect the hull from outside first

Without boarding, scan hull sides, rub rail, transom, swim platform, windows, rails, antennas and covers. Look for new scuffs aligned with dock hardware, cracks, distortion, puncture, displaced through-hull fittings, a changed waterline, listing or settling. Check neighboring boats and floating debris without entering the water.

Never swim under or around a storm-damaged boat. Propulsion, entanglement, electricity, contamination and unstable structures can make the water unsafe. If the boat is low, listing, leaking or pinned, call the marina and a qualified salvage or marine service provider. The taking-on-water response helps identify immediate priorities, but active sinking is an emergency, not a DIY inspection.

Board only when the transfer is controlled

Confirm the boat is secured, propulsion cannot start unexpectedly and the step is within normal safe geometry. Maintain three points of contact and carry no loose load during transfer. A second adult remains in a safe location and knows the plan. Do not jump a new gap or cross a boat that is heeling, grounded under load or moving against damaged hardware.

Once aboard, pause. Listen for pumps, alarms, hissing and shifting. Smell for fuel or electrical overheating without deliberately inhaling vapors. If there is a strong fuel odor, visible leak, smoke, heat or unexplained water rise, leave without operating switches and call emergency or qualified help as appropriate.

Measure water before using the bilge pump

Photograph the bilge level, high-water marks and contents before changing them. Compare with the boat's normal state. Identify whether rain entered through a cover, a fitting failed or hull damage is possible; do not assume storm water is harmless. Check accessible compartments and limber paths without dismantling systems beyond your competence.

Do not energize a pump when wiring, batteries, fuel vapor or discharge legality is uncertain. Oily or fuel-contaminated water must not be pumped overboard. The EPA and National Response Center provide spill-reporting routes, while local authorities may add requirements. Use absorbents only when it is safe and they are appropriate; they do not make a leak repaired.

Keep fuel and electricity de-energized

Inspect visible fuel fills, vents, hoses, tanks and surrounding spaces for displacement, wetness, odor and impact without opening a system or creating ignition. Do not start engines, operate blowers, cycle battery switches or plug in chargers merely to “see what works” when fuel or electrical damage is possible. Eliminate flames and smoking; keep people away.

Inspect accessible batteries for movement, cracked cases, submerged terminals and damaged restraints without touching compromised components. Saltwater or contaminated-water immersion can create delayed corrosion and electrical hazards. Follow the boat, battery, charger and engine manufacturers' emergency instructions. Use an ABYC-certified or otherwise qualified marine technician when circuit protection, bonding, shore power, fuel, ventilation or battery integrity is uncertain.

Treat propulsion and steering as impact systems

From a safe external position, look for a bent propeller, displaced drive, damaged skeg, line entanglement, leaking lubricant, shifted outboard mounting, impact marks and debris around intakes. Do not place hands near a propeller or between moving components, and do not trim or rotate equipment until the boat manual and scene make that safe.

At the helm, check whether steering and controls show obvious displacement without forcing them. Grounding, collision or debris strike can damage components that still move at the dock. Do not run the engine to diagnose vibration or cooling after a suspected strike. A qualified technician should inspect propulsion, mounts, steering and underwater gear before operation when impact evidence exists. Use the grounding response plan for a documented strike.

Document before cleanup

Create one sequence: wide scene, access path, every side of the boat, lines and cleats, waterline, bilge, controls, damaged property, serial or hull identification and close-ups with scale. Record date, time, weather source, marina notices, water level, witnesses and actions taken. Preserve receipts and written instructions. Do not make unsafe access for a better photo.

Contact the insurer promptly using the policy's claim route and follow instructions about mitigation, survey, salvage and repair. The boat insurance guide explains coverage questions but cannot interpret a specific claim. Prevent additional damage only when safe and authorized; retain replaced parts when instructed. Do not erase load evidence or authorize structural repair before the responsible parties can document it.

Earn the first start

A first start happens only after red and yellow conditions are cleared, required repairs or inspections are complete, the bilge and fuel spaces are safe, batteries and wiring are intact, propulsion is unobstructed and the boat-specific manual's pre-start procedure is satisfied. Ventilate gasoline-engine spaces exactly as required. Confirm cooling-water supply and keep suitable fire protection available.

Use a controlled dockside check with one operator, one observer and a clear shutdown threshold. Stop for alarm, fuel or exhaust odor, smoke, heat, abnormal vibration, steering or shift problem, charging fault, unusual discharge or rising water. Do not leave the slip as the diagnostic test. A technician's clearance and a calm short verification plan may be required before operation.

Close with a written release, not relief

Record each inspected system as pass, unresolved or referred—never “probably fine.” Schedule rechecks because wet connectors, absorbed water, chafe and corrosion can appear later. Reinspect lines, bilge level, batteries and repaired covers after the next rain and before the next departure. Update the boat logbook and keep photos with the boat record.

Anyone should stop the recovery when a marina closure, utility hazard, unstable structure, fuel issue, active leak, damaged electrical system, propulsion strike or skill boundary appears. The protocol succeeds when it prevents an unsafe first touch or first start. Cosmetic cleanup is last. People, scene control, evidence and system integrity come first—in that order.

Frequently asked questions

Can I board my boat as soon as a storm passes?

Not automatically. Confirm official and marina access, assess the dock and utilities from land, and stay off any unstable, energized, flooded or closed area.

Should I turn on the bilge pump after a storm?

Only after electrical and fuel conditions are safe and the water is legal to discharge. Do not pump oily or fuel-contaminated water overboard; use official spill-reporting guidance.

Can I start the engine to see whether it was damaged?

Not when water intrusion, fuel, electrical, propulsion or impact damage is uncertain. Document first and follow the boat and engine manuals plus qualified technician direction.

What should I photograph for an insurance claim?

Capture the wide scene, access path, every side of the boat, lines, cleats, waterline, bilge, controls, identification and detailed damage, with date, time and actions recorded.
