Boat Trailer Tongue Jacks: Single Wheel vs Dual Wheel vs Fixed Foot

The right jack lifts the coupler through the required range and survives the ramp environment. The wrong one can drag, fold, roll or simply refuse to fit the tongue.

A boat-trailer tongue jack has a narrow job: raise and lower the coupler without exceeding the jack, bracket or trailer limits. It is not a parking brake, a service stand or a license to move a loaded trailer across a slope. Selection begins with measured tongue load, then moves through mounting geometry, lift range, surface and corrosion exposure. That sequence matters because a high-capacity jack that cannot retract above the road, reach the hitch or fit the frame is still the wrong jack.

Start with tongue load—not the boat's brochure weight

The jack carries the trailer's tongue load, not the full boat-and-trailer weight. But that does not make a generic percentage or a dry hull number an adequate selection. Fuel, batteries, anchors, gear, engine position and loading can change the real force at the coupler. Measure the trailer in its normal ready-to-tow condition with an appropriate tongue-weight scale or follow a verified weighing method, then compare the result with the trailer and jack manufacturers' requirements.

Leave capacity margin according to the manufacturers' guidance, and make sure the mount, fasteners and tongue structure are rated for the same job. A larger number cannot repair a cracked weld, corroded frame or thin improvised bracket. If the trailer's original specification is missing or the measured load is surprising, stop and involve the trailer builder or a qualified trailer technician.

Measure the heights the jack must actually reach

Park the loaded trailer on firm, level ground, chock the wheels and level the frame as the manufacturer specifies. Measure the coupler height in the level-towing position and the tow vehicle's hitch-ball height under its normal load. Then document the jack's bracket height, retracted height, extended height, usable travel and any drop-leg adjustment. The jack must retract far enough for safe road clearance and extend far enough to clear the ball without living at the end of its stroke.

Venture Trailers' current guidance treats retracted and extended dimensions as separate checks. That is the useful model. A jack with impressive travel can still hang too low after it swings up, while a compact jack can run out of extension on a tall tow vehicle. Confirm handle clearance from the bow, winch stand, spare tire and coupler through the complete motion.

A single wheel fits routine movement on good pavement

A swivel jack with one caster wheel is the familiar boat-trailer default because it can roll a light tongue a short distance on smooth, level, hard ground. That mobility helps align a coupler by inches, not relocate a loaded trailer across a driveway. Small wheels dig into gravel, catch pavement seams and can rotate sideways under load. They also make slope movement easier, which is a liability when the trailer should remain still.

Compare single-wheel trailer jacks on Amazon only after measuring load, travel, frame size and road clearance. Reject vague listings that omit retracted length, bracket dimensions, fastener requirements or a current manual.

Dual wheels spread contact; they do not create a tow vehicle

Two wheels can widen the contact patch and may roll more predictably on a firm surface, especially when the tongue load would make one narrow caster sink or bind. They also add width, hardware and potential interference. Twin casters can still stop abruptly in a rut, scrub sideways, roll downhill or overload their pivot. Do not treat them as off-road tires.

Compare dual-wheel trailer jacks on Amazon by documented wheel rating, bracket fit and clearance. If a powered trailer mover is the real need, define that different system separately; a dual-wheel manual jack is not a substitute for a rated mover, controlled surface and safe operating procedure.

A fixed foot favors stability over hand positioning

A fixed-foot or drop-leg jack places a broad pad on the ground rather than a caster. It is usually the cleaner choice when the trailer should stay put, the surface is compacted gravel or soil, or tongue load makes casual rolling undesirable. A drop leg can reduce cranking by placing the foot near the ground before lifting, but only when its pin is fully engaged and the manufacturer's overlap and loading requirements are satisfied.

Compare fixed-foot trailer jacks on Amazon after checking lift range, pin retention and pad area. Use a sound, appropriately sized jack pad where the manual permits it. Never stack loose blocks to manufacture height.

The mount is a compatibility decision, not a drill pattern

Record the tongue's width, height, shape and available straight section. Confirm bolt spacing, clamp range, swing radius, stowed orientation and interference with wiring, brake lines, safety chains, the winch post and bow. Some trailers use proprietary brackets. Dutton-Lainson specifically warns that certain Ranger trailers use a unique center-swing bracket, which is a useful reminder that a generic swivel mount does not fit every trailer that looks similar in a photograph.

Do not drill the tongue, relocate brake components or improvise spacers without written manufacturer approval. Corrosion around old holes or welds deserves structural inspection before a new, heavier jack is installed. The boat-trailer safety checklist covers the rest of the tow system.

Plan for water, salt and maintenance

Galvanized or otherwise corrosion-resistant construction is appropriate for wet service, but no finish makes gears, fasteners and pivots maintenance-free. Follow the jack manual for lubrication points, intervals and acceptable products. Rinse salt and ramp grime without forcing water into protected mechanisms. Inspect the tube, foot or wheel, axle, pivot, handle, gears, welds, clamp plates, bolts and retaining pins before towing.

A jack that binds, leans, drops under load, has a bent tube or will not lock fully should come out of service. Do not keep forcing the crank. Record the make, model, rated capacity, installation date and manual in the boat records file so the next owner does not have to guess.

Use the jack without becoming the load path

Chock the trailer wheels before uncoupling. Keep feet, hands and children away from the coupler and jack path. Raise only enough to clear the hitch, verify the coupler and safety chains are released, and keep the trailer from rolling. When reconnecting, fully latch and verify the coupler, attach crossed safety chains and the breakaway system where fitted, raise or swing the jack completely into its locked travel position, and perform a walk-around.

Never work beneath or put body parts under a trailer supported only by its tongue jack. For service, use the trailer manufacturer's approved lifting points and properly rated stands on a suitable surface. The launch sequence and retrieval sequence keep ramp pressure from replacing checks.

What not to buy—and when not to buy anything

  • A jack selected from total trailer weight without measuring or resolving tongue load.
  • A wheel jack for routine use on slopes, loose gravel, mud or broken ground.
  • A mount that requires unexplained drilling, stacked spacers or contact with brake lines.
  • A model whose retracted height, lift range, frame fit or manual is unavailable.
  • A powered jack without a documented electrical and manual-override plan.

The no-buy option is real. If the current jack is correctly rated, structurally sound, fully retracts, reaches the hitch, locks securely and only feels rough, manufacturer-approved cleaning, lubrication or a replaceable wheel may solve the problem. Replace uncertainty, not hardware by reflex.

Frequently asked questions

How much capacity should a boat-trailer tongue jack have?

Use the measured loaded tongue weight plus the margin required by the jack and trailer manufacturers. Do not select from boat length, dry hull weight or a generic percentage alone.

Is a dual-wheel trailer jack better than a single wheel?

It can spread contact and roll more predictably on firm, level ground, but it adds width and does not make a loaded trailer safe to move on slopes or rough ground.

Is a wheel or a fixed foot safer?

A fixed foot generally favors stability; a wheel favors small positioning movements on hard, level pavement. The correct choice depends on the measured load, surface, clearance and manufacturer guidance.

Can I drill the trailer tongue for a new jack?

Only with written approval for that trailer structure and installation. Drilling can weaken or damage the tongue and may interfere with brake lines or wiring.

Can I work under a trailer supported by its jack?

No. Use manufacturer-approved lifting points and properly rated stands on a suitable surface. A tongue jack is not a service stand.
