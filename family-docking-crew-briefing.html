The Family Docking Brief: Seats, Hands, Lines, and One Voice

Docking is not the moment to invent jobs. Brief in open water: children seated, hands and feet inside, one adult on lines, one operator giving commands—and no shame in going around.

By Nautical Dream Editorial DeskUpdated September 4, 202614 minute read

A family docking problem usually becomes visible in the last boat length, but it often begins minutes earlier: no agreed side, a line still in a locker, a child sent to fend off, two adults giving opposite instructions or a passenger preparing to jump. A useful brief removes those decisions before the operator enters the marina or ramp lane. It does not make every docking easy; it makes the crew predictable enough for the operator to abort safely.

Brief before the dock becomes urgent

Slow in open water with room to maneuver and explain the plan in one minute: which side will meet the dock, where each person sits, who handles which line, where fenders go, what words the operator will use and what happens on a go-around. The operator keeps responsibility for the vessel and can cancel the approach at any point. No passenger should interpret proximity as permission to move.

Check wind, current, traffic, dock height, cleats, hazards and the departure path if the first approach fails. Prepare lines and fenders while the boat is stable, not while the operator is correcting a crosswind. If the docking geometry or conditions exceed the operator's training, wait, choose another berth or request competent assistance.

Give children the most important non-job

A child's role is to stay in the assigned seat, keep the life jacket properly fastened, keep hands and feet inside and wait for the adult release. Children do not catch the dock, push pilings, jump with a line, hold a fender or move onto the bow. A small hand cannot stop the mass of a moving boat; a foot between hull and dock can be crushed before an adult reacts.

Use a short script: “Bottom on the seat. Jacket on. Hands in. Wait for ‘all secure.’” Give a nervous child a safe object to hold or a lookout task away from the docking side, but do not create an operational duty whose failure could harm someone. Federal law requires children under 13 to wear a USCG-approved life jacket while underway, subject to stated enclosed-cabin exceptions; state rules may add requirements.

One operator owns speed, gear and commands

The person at the helm controls the approach and gives the operational words. Other adults can report distance or hazards, but they should not issue independent throttle or steering commands. Agree on plain language such as “stand by,” “line ready,” “step ashore,” “hold,” “release” and “abort.” A shouted sentence is too slow when one word will do.

Use the engine cut-off link where required and appropriate. Keep the operator seated or braced at the controls until the boat is secure and the propulsion system is safe. Never leave the helm to grab a rail while the engine is in gear. The single-handed docking guide is the better model when no trained line handler is available.

One adult handles lines without becoming a human fender

The line handler prepares only the lines the plan needs, led clear of railings, feet and loose equipment. A bitter end stays aboard unless the plan specifically requires otherwise. Coils remain controlled, and no one wraps a line around a hand, wrist, arm or body. The handler steps onto a safe surface only after the operator says to and only when the gap and height permit a normal step—not a leap.

If the dock cannot be reached safely, the handler reports “not made” and stays aboard. They do not stretch, jump or try to stop the boat by pulling against momentum. A boat hook may extend reach when used from a stable position, but it is not a pole vault or brake. The operator then goes around.

Rig fenders and lines where they solve the actual contact

Choose fender height from the dock or piling, not from habit. A floating dock, fixed pier and lock wall need different placement. Secure fenders to strong, appropriate points before the close approach and keep them from blocking the operator's sightline or falling into the propulsion area. A child should never be asked to hold a fender by hand between hull and dock.

Prepare a line that can control the boat from the intended cleat without crossing passengers or entering the propeller zone. Spring lines can control fore-and-aft movement, but their sequence depends on wind, current, drive type and docking geometry. Practice with an instructor or experienced operator rather than learning a high-load line technique from a generic article.

The dock-line guide covers material, diameter and safe handling.

Keep the propeller zone physically boring

No person enters the water, sits on the swim platform, uses a boarding ladder or reaches near an outboard or sterndrive while propulsion could engage. Keep loose lines, clothing and hair away from the machinery. Before anyone uses the platform or water, shut down and disable starting according to the manufacturer's procedure; neutral alone is not a guard.

USCG guidance emphasizes rules for swim-platform use, ladders and seating and explains why an engine cut-off device matters if the operator is displaced. During docking, the same discipline prevents a line or person from entering the propeller area. If a line goes overboard near the drive, shift to neutral, stop the engine when safe and resolve it without putting a person beside machinery.

Normalize the go-around before it is needed

A go-around is not failure. It is the standard response when the angle, speed, wind, current, traffic, line, fender or crew position is wrong. Say “abort,” ensure everyone stays seated and inside, use neutral or the minimum controlled power needed for safe separation, regain open water and reset. The exact maneuver depends on the boat's handling and nearby hazards.

Do not add throttle merely to rescue a poor plan. Do not let a passenger jump because the dock is almost reachable. Do not accept a hand from shore unless the plan and boat position make it safe; a well-meaning helper can pull the bow or stern the wrong way. The operator can decline assistance and make another approach.

Change the brief for wind, current and visibility

Wind and current can move the boat sideways faster than the crew can respond. Identify which force dominates at the dock, where the boat will drift in neutral and which direction offers a safe escape. Put the first useful line and fenders on the contact side, but never commit the boat to a position the operator cannot leave. Strong crosswind, current, darkness, fog or a crowded fuel dock may justify waiting or choosing another landing.

At night, preserve the operator's vision and keep portable beams out of faces and windshields; use the boat-lighting guide. In restricted visibility, docking urgency does not cancel lookout or safe-speed duties. One adult may need to become a dedicated lookout, leaving no trained line handler. That is a reason to wait or request help.

Secure, shut down and release the family in order

After the first line is made, the boat may still move. The operator stays at the controls while the trained adult completes the agreed lines. Confirm the boat is secure for the expected wake, water-level change and time alongside. Shift to neutral and shut down according to the boat and engine instructions before anyone moves near the stern, swim platform or dock edge.

Only then say “all secure” and release children from their seats one at a time. Keep life jackets on around the dock as conditions and law require. Pass bags after people are stable so no one combines a gap crossing with a cooler or backpack. On departure, reverse the sequence: passengers aboard and seated, lines managed, route clear, then propulsion enabled.

Practice the brief where mistakes stay small

Rehearse the words and seating plan at home, then practice in calm open water and at an uncrowded dock with a qualified instructor or experienced operator. Let each adult demonstrate coiling, cleating and releasing a line without wrapping it around the body. Practice a deliberate go-around more often than a heroic catch.

Stop and seek instruction when the operator cannot control the boat at idle, the drive behaves unexpectedly in reverse, the dock requires a high-load spring line, or conditions leave no safe escape. Bow thrusters and electronic controls do not replace a predictable crew.

Before approach

  • Dock side, wind/current and escape path identified
  • Children seated, jackets fastened and limbs inside
  • One operator and one line handler named

On command

  • No jumping, pushing or human fenders
  • Abort means stay seated and reset
  • All secure comes before passengers move

Frequently asked questions

What should children do while a boat is docking?

Stay in an assigned seat with the life jacket fastened, keep hands and feet inside and wait for an adult to say the boat is secure. Children should not catch the dock, push off, hold fenders or jump with lines.

Should someone jump from the boat to the dock with a line?

No. The line handler should step only when the operator directs and the surface, height and gap allow a normal safe step. If the dock is not reachable, stay aboard and go around.

Can a passenger push the boat away from the dock with a foot?

No. Hands, feet and bodies are not fenders. Use properly placed fenders, controlled lines and another approach.

Who gives commands during family docking?

One operator should own speed, gear and operational commands. Other adults report hazards and handle assigned lines without issuing conflicting directions.

When should a boater abandon a docking approach?

Abort when angle, speed, wind, current, traffic, line setup, fenders or crew position is wrong. Regain open water and reset rather than adding speed or asking someone to jump.
