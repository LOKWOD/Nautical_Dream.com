Shore-power failures happen at interfaces: a cord is the right color but the wrong rating, an adapter makes incompatible hardware appear usable, a locking ring is missing, or resistance at a tired connection becomes heat. Buying safely starts before shopping. Photograph the boat inlet and marina receptacle, copy every published rating, identify the connection family, and reject any solution that depends on guessing what is behind either faceplate.

Write the five-line shore-power fit record

Boat inlet: record the exact manufacturer, model or documented connection family, amperage and voltage from the plate and manual. Dock outlet: record the receptacle configuration and published rating from the marina, not from a distant photo. Electrical: write volts, amps and whether the system is 125V or 125/250V. Connection: note twist-lock, locking ring or the exact proprietary system. Length: measure the safe route without stretching, splicing or leaving coils where they create trip or heat problems.

Keep those five lines together. “Thirty amp” alone is not a complete fit description, and a plug that can be physically forced is not proof of electrical compatibility. Do not copy a neighboring boat’s arrangement. The shore-power pedestal safety guide covers the arrival inspection; this guide covers the portable cordset between verified interfaces.

Understand the common ratings without treating them as interchangeable

Recreational marinas commonly offer 30A/125V and 50A/125/250V service, but the published markings on the boat, pedestal and cordset decide the real case. A 50-amp cord is not simply a thicker upgrade for a 30-amp inlet. Its conductors, connector geometry, voltage arrangement, overcurrent assumptions and weight are different. Likewise, an apparently similar 50A/125V component is not automatically a substitute for 50A/125/250V equipment.

Match the complete rating at both ends and in the boat manual. Never file a blade, remove a pin, defeat a locking feature or rewire a connector to make it fit. If labels are unreadable, the inlet has been altered, or a previous owner left an unexplained adapter chain, stop and have a qualified marine electrical technician identify the system. The marine wiring limits guide explains why an external plug is not the place to infer an internal circuit.

Buy a complete marine cordset, not an extension-cord theory

The U.S. Coast Guard’s shore-power guidance says cords should be suitable for marine use and warns against ordinary outdoor extension cords. It calls for locking connections, a weatherproof molded or booted shore plug, and a locking ring at the boat connector. Look for the exact marine-use listing or certification documented by the manufacturer, molded ratings that remain readable, strain relief at both ends and replacement support for the connection system.

Choose enough length for a relaxed, protected route from boat to pedestal with a small service allowance. Do not buy excess length merely to reach every slip; long heavy cords are harder to route, easier to damage and tempting to leave coiled. One correctly sized cordset is preferable to improvised household extensions. If two manufacturer-approved marine cordsets must be joined, follow the exact instructions and keep the connection protected as directed.

Use adapters only after a documented load decision

An adapter changes connector geometry; it does not create capacity, correct voltage or repair a weak connection. The Coast Guard guidance places adapters at the shore end of the cord. Before any adapter is considered, the boat manual, adapter manufacturer and marina must all support the exact source, load and connection. A boat wired for more available current can overload a lower-rated shore source unless loads are deliberately managed within the approved arrangement.

Reject homemade “dogbones,” daisy chains, unlabeled splitters and any device whose neutral, grounding or voltage arrangement is unclear. Never use an adapter to bypass a marina breaker, ground-fault device, locking feature or damaged receptacle. The safe answer to an incompatible pedestal may be another slip. The home-marina guide makes shore-power service and support part of the berth decision rather than an arrival surprise.

Inspect cold, clean and disconnected

Before each connection, with the system de-energized under the exact boat and marina sequence, inspect the jacket for cuts, crushing, severe abrasion, stiffness and repairs. Inspect both connector bodies, blades, contacts, locking hardware and strain reliefs for cracks, looseness, bending, corrosion, soot, discoloration, melting or odor. Confirm everything is clean and dry. A cord end dropped in water is not made safe by shaking it off.

Also inspect the boat inlet and ask the marina to address a damaged pedestal receptacle. A new cord cannot cure a corroded inlet or loose dock outlet. SmartPlug’s current manufacturer guidance identifies melting, discoloration, corrosion and loose connections as replacement or service triggers. ABYC’s voltage-drop explanation connects loose or corroded connections with added resistance and heat. Heat is evidence to investigate, not a normal trait to monitor indefinitely.

Connect and disconnect in the published order

Follow the exact cordset, boat and marina instructions. The Coast Guard circular advises turning the boat’s main breaker off, connecting the female cord end to the boat first, fully locking and sealing it, then connecting at the shore pedestal. For disconnection, it reverses the live end first: turn off power as directed, disconnect at shore, then at the boat. Never leave a cord live from the pedestal with the boat end loose on the dock.

Route the cable where it will not be pinched by cleats, doors, dock movement or boarding traffic. Do not suspend it from the plug, sharply bend it at strain reliefs or submerge a connection. Keep the marina’s emergency and reporting process available. The transient-slip guide helps obtain pedestal details before arrival instead of solving them beside an energized dock.

Use a temperature trend as a stop tool, not a safety certificate

After the system has operated at a normal documented load, a cord and connection should not show abnormal heat, odor, noise or discoloration. Without opening equipment or touching suspect conductors, an infrared thermometer can help compare the same visible point over time. It cannot see a hidden loose terminal, prove a safe connection or replace a qualified inspection. Emissivity, distance and reflective surfaces can make readings misleading.

Stop use and de-energize safely if a plug, inlet or receptacle becomes unusually warm or hot, smells burned, buzzes, arcs, discolors, loses power or trips protection repeatedly. Do not reset a breaker repeatedly, tighten energized hardware or wrap a damaged connector. Ask the marina to handle its pedestal and a qualified marine electrician to handle boat-side faults. The marine fire-extinguisher guide supports emergency readiness; it does not make continued operation acceptable.

Retire the cord instead of normalizing damage

Retire a cordset for cracked or badly abraded jacket, exposed conductors, loose or bent blades, failed locking hardware, melted or discolored bodies, burned odor, corrosion, repeated overheating or any defect the manufacturer identifies. Do not tape over structural damage, replace a connector casually or transfer a suspect cord to a lower-load job. A field-installed end is an electrical repair governed by the exact manufacturer instructions and applicable standards.

Photograph the retired cord’s ratings and damage, cut power before removal, and mark it so nobody reconnects it. Record the replacement model, rating, length and first commissioning check in the maintenance evidence file. If the old connection heated, the mating inlet and pedestal also need evaluation; replacing only the portable cord may leave the cause in service.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use a 30-amp cord on a 50-amp boat?

Not by assumption. Connector, voltage, amperage, load management, adapter and boat-manufacturer requirements all have to support the exact arrangement. Ask the marina and a qualified marine electrical technician when the record is incomplete.

Is a warm shore-power plug normal?

Unusual warmth, rising temperature, odor, discoloration or looseness is a stop-and-investigate condition. De-energize safely and have the appropriate marina or marine electrical professional inspect the system.

Can I repair a cracked shore-power cord with tape?

No. Tape does not restore a marine cordset’s jacket, strain relief, listing or connector integrity. Retire damaged equipment and follow the manufacturer’s replacement or qualified-repair guidance.
