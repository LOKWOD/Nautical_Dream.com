An inflatable life jacket is a system: approved bladder and cover, inflator, cylinder, activation parts, oral tube, status indicators and maintenance instructions. The U.S. Coast Guard says an inflatable must be maintained according to the manufacturer, with a full cylinder and all status indicators green, or it is not serviceable. That makes the label and manual—not a marketplace photo—the buying authority. This guide builds a durable fit card for finding the exact kit and deciding whether rearming is enough.

Start with the approval label and model

Open the jacket exactly as its instructions permit and photograph the approval label, manufacturer, model, serial or lot information, size and intended-use limitations. Record the rearming-kit number if the label or manual provides one. Mustang Survival's current rearm-kit collection includes a model-based finder; Onyx directs owners to the product manual for the proper kit.

Do not shop from color, collar shape or the cylinder already installed. Covers can look alike while using different inflators or charge sizes, and a prior owner may already have fitted the wrong part. If the label is missing, unreadable or inconsistent with the inflator, contact the manufacturer with photographs or retire the device under its guidance. The life-jacket selection guide separates this service question from choosing a PFD for the activity.

Identify the inflation architecture

Record whether the jacket is manual-only, automatic or hydrostatic, and identify the exact inflator family named by the maker. Manual systems normally rely on a pull handle; automatic systems add a water-sensitive element; hydrostatic systems use a pressure-related mechanism. Those descriptions explain categories, not interchangeability. A cylinder, cap, bobbin or inflator from one architecture may not belong in another.

Some kits contain only a cylinder and activation element; others include an inflator body or cap, indicators and a service key. Match the complete bill of materials to the current manual. Do not convert an automatic model to manual-only, mix inflator generations or substitute a generic water-sensing element unless the PFD manufacturer expressly approves that exact configuration.

Cylinder fit is more than thread fit

Record the specified gas charge, cylinder part number, thread or bayonet interface, neck geometry, sealing surface and any required indicator or retaining hardware. The gram value matters because the bladder and approval were designed around a particular charge. A larger cylinder is not an upgrade, and a smaller one can leave the jacket underinflated.

A cylinder that screws into the mechanism can still have the wrong charge or sealing geometry. Never weigh, refill, drill or adapt a disposable cylinder, and never use a damaged, corroded, discharged or punctured one. Automatic elements have dates and storage rules

An automatic inflator may use a dissolvable bobbin, cap or other activation element with a printed manufacture or replacement date. A hydrostatic unit can have a service or expiry date for the inflator assembly. Record the date, replacement interval and storage limits exactly as the manual states. A new cylinder does not renew an expired activation component.

Moisture, spray, high humidity and improper storage can affect automatic parts before the calendar date. Do not dry and reuse a triggered element unless the manufacturer explicitly permits it. Keep spare kits sealed as directed, and record their dates so the onboard spare does not quietly age out. Inspect the jacket before spending on a kit

Rearming replaces consumed or time-limited components; it does not repair a leaking bladder, torn cover, damaged oral tube, separated seam, corroded fitting or contaminated inflator. Follow the maker's pre-wear inspection and leak-test procedure. Do not invent pressure, soap-solution or overnight tests that the manual does not specify.

Inspect webbing, buckles, pull handle, reflective material and attachment points as well as the inflation hardware. If the device was submerged while stowed, inflated violently, exposed to fuel or solvents, or shows mildew or abrasion, ask the manufacturer or a qualified PFD service provider whether it can return to service. A cheap kit is poor value when the underlying approved device should be replaced.

Rearm in the maker's sequence

Work in a clean, dry area with the correct manual and unopened kit. Remove the spent parts only in the documented order. Confirm that the new components match the fit card, check seals and dates, and install them with the specified orientation and hand-tool procedure. Do not add tape, sealant, lubricant or torque unless the instructions call for it.

Reset the pull mechanism and indicators exactly as shown, route the lanyard without tangles, and repack the bladder using the prescribed folds. A pull handle hidden inside the cover or a bladder trapped by a zipper defeats the system. Return-to-service needs more than a green window

After rearming, complete every inspection in the PFD manual: cylinder engagement, indicator state, manual lever position, oral valve, cover closure and any specified inflation or leak check. The Coast Guard's serviceability rule requires a full cylinder and all status indicators green, but the absence of visible damage and correct fit for the wearer and activity still matter.

Record the date, kit number, component dates and result. Put the device on and adjust it on land according to the instructions; a technically armed jacket left under a seat is not protection. The predeparture checklist keeps the indicator and wear decision visible every trip.

Carry a spare only if it is the right spare

An onboard spare should be the exact approved kit for each inflatable model carried, sealed and stored dry within the maker's temperature and humidity limits. If two jackets use different kits, label the storage bags by exact model without covering the original packaging. A box of mixed cylinders is not readiness.

Also carry wearable inherently buoyant PFDs appropriate to the people and activity when the operating plan or regulations require them. Inflatable PFDs have age, activity and wear limitations; they are not a universal substitute for children's life jackets, weak swimmers or high-impact activities. What not to buy—and when to retire

Skip a kit described as universal without an exact manufacturer cross-reference. Skip loose cylinders without traceable specifications, opened activation parts, expired components and marketplace bundles that omit the maker's kit number. Do not buy by cylinder gram value alone, and do not treat a green indicator photo as evidence of current serviceability.

Stop for an unreadable approval label, mismatched inflator, corroded mechanism, damaged bladder or oral valve, failed leak test, missing instructions or unavailable approved parts. Contact the manufacturer or a qualified marine safety-equipment service. The correct conclusion may be a documented kit, professional inspection or replacement of the entire PFD—not a clever combination of parts.

Frequently asked questions

Are inflatable life-jacket rearming kits universal?

No. Match the exact PFD model, inflator architecture, cylinder specification, activation parts and manufacturer-listed kit number.

Can I use a larger CO2 cylinder for more buoyancy?

No. Use only the cylinder charge and interface specified by the PFD manufacturer for that approved device.

Is a green indicator enough to prove an inflatable PFD is serviceable?

No. It also needs a full correct cylinder, all required inspections, no damage, correct maintenance, and suitability for the wearer and activity.

Should I rearm a jacket with a missing label?

Do not guess. Ask the manufacturer whether it can be identified and serviced; otherwise retire it under qualified guidance.
