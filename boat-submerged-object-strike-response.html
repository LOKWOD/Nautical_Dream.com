Underwater-impact response

You Hit Something Underwater: Stop, Inspect, and Do Not Trust the Propeller

The engine may still run. The boat may still steer. Neither proves the hull, lower unit, shaft, propeller or steering system survived the impact well enough for normal speed.

By Nautical Dream Editorial DeskUpdated September 3, 202615 minute read

A submerged-object strike can range from a light propeller nick to structural damage that opens a leak, distorts steering or compromises a shaft, gearcase, transom or hull. The sound and the operator's first glance do not reliably rank the damage. The safe response reduces power, stabilizes people and position, checks for flooding and loss of control, communicates early and treats any abnormal vibration or uncertainty as a reason to stop using the propulsion system.

Get off the throttle without creating a second collision

Bring the throttle back promptly and maintain only the control needed to avoid traffic, shore, shoals or another hazard. Warn the crew, seat everyone and confirm fitted life jackets are on. Attach the engine cut-off switch link as required if the engine must run for immediate maneuvering. Keep a lookout on the object, nearby vessels and the boat's drift.

Do not make an abrupt turn toward people, paddlers or rocks just to return to the impact point. Do not reverse hard over an unknown obstruction. Once the boat has safe water and room, shift to neutral and shut down according to the engine instructions. A strike in a channel, current, surf or commercial traffic may require a different stabilization move than a strike on a quiet lake. Preserve control first, then inspect.

The navigation-rules guide still governs the surrounding traffic problem.

Check people before machinery

Ask each person directly about falls, impact, pain and head contact. A passenger may have been thrown against a seat or rail even when the hull shows no obvious damage. Keep children seated and calm, account for every person and look for someone who entered the water or lost needed medication or communication equipment.

Use first aid within the crew's training. Call for emergency assistance when injury, person overboard, uncontrolled drift or immediate danger exists. Do not delay a distress call while trying to produce a perfect mechanical diagnosis. Give position, vessel description, people aboard and the nature of the problem plainly.

Assign one adult to passengers and communication so the operator is not simultaneously opening hatches, watching traffic and calming children. The VHF distress-call practice makes that division usable before an emergency.

Look for water immediately—and keep looking

Open only safe, accessible hatches and inspect bilges, lockers, transom areas, through-hulls, shaft logs and compartments near the impact. Listen for rushing or trickling water. Check the bilge-pump indicator and high-water alarm, but do not interpret pump noise as proof that the boat is keeping up. Verify actual discharge where it can be observed safely.

Compare the water level after a short interval. A slow rise is still a leak. Identify the source only when it can be reached without entering machinery spaces, moving under unsupported components or exposing the crew to fuel, electrical or entanglement hazards. Use damage-control materials only as trained and appropriate.

If water is entering faster than the system controls it, make the distress call, prepare flotation and move toward the safest reachable refuge without sacrificing the ability to abandon if necessary. Follow the taking-on-water response.

Do not touch the propeller until the engine cannot start

Before any close inspection, shut the engine down, remove the key or disable starting as the manufacturer directs, and confirm every person knows not to operate controls. Keep hands, hair, clothing and lines away from the propeller. Do not enter the water beside the boat to inspect damage unless a trained emergency response makes that unavoidable; current, cold water, waves and an unstable hull can turn inspection into rescue.

For a trailerable outboard or sterndrive, trim only as the manufacturer's instructions and conditions allow. Do not force a damaged trim system or raise hardware where it could contact the hull. From aboard, look for missing or bent blades, fishing line, a damaged skeg, gearcase cracks, oil sheen, loose mounting, transom movement and steering components out of alignment.

A clean-looking propeller does not clear the shaft, hub, gears, bearings or hull.

The no-vibration rule is the practical boundary

If the boat is not taking water and the propulsion system must be used to reach immediate safety, restart only according to the manufacturer and use the minimum power that preserves control. Stop at once for vibration, knocking, grinding, unusual exhaust, cooling-water loss, steering change, gear-engagement trouble, oil alarm, visible leak or transom movement. Do not accelerate through vibration to see whether it smooths out.

A bent propeller blade or shaft can create loads that damage seals, bearings, gears and mounts. A spun hub can allow the engine to rev without producing normal thrust. A lower unit that shifts now may still have internal damage. Mercury's owner guidance emphasizes visual propeller and prop-shaft inspection and its manuals direct inspection after impact; the exact procedure depends on the propulsion model.

If a tow can be arranged safely, it is often cheaper than converting uncertain damage into a destroyed gearcase or lost control.

Hull, drive and steering damage are connected

Outboards and sterndrives transmit impact through mounting brackets, trim systems and the transom. Inboards can transmit it through the propeller, shaft, strut, bearings and hull structure. Jet drives have different intake, impeller and steering risks. Sailboats add keel, rudder and internal-grid concerns. A universal propeller-only checklist cannot clear every boat.

Inspect accessible structure for new cracking, displaced sealant, wet cores, loose fasteners, changing alignment, fuel odor and electrical damage. Cycle steering only enough to confirm safe control and stop if it binds, jumps, leaks or no longer centers normally. Hydraulic steering damage may not announce itself with a dramatic puddle.

Use a qualified marine technician or surveyor when the strike was significant, the object or speed is unknown, vibration exists, the hull or transom shows change, a shaft seal may be affected or the operator cannot inspect the load path.

Mark the position and warn without improvising

Save the GPS position and note time, depth, speed, heading, weather, water level and what was felt or heard. If the object remains a hazard to navigation, report it to the appropriate local authority or Coast Guard channel with an accurate position and description. Do not linger in danger or make repeated passes trying to identify it.

Do not move, tow or mark an object with a loose line that could foul your propeller or another vessel. A floating log may drift; a charted rock will not. Use current charts and local notices to distinguish a newly observed hazard from a known obstruction, but do not assume the chart is wrong because the operator believed the route was clear.

Preserve photographs and notes for service, insurance and any required accident report. Facts recorded immediately are more useful than a confident reconstruction at the dock.

Return, tow or stop: choose by consequence

Return under own power only when people are well, the boat is dry and stable, steering and cooling are normal, no vibration or leak exists, and the route is short, protected and compatible with minimum power. Continue monitoring bilges, temperature, alarms and steering.

Request assistance or a tow when propulsion condition is uncertain, vibration appears, steering changes, the route crosses open water or the boat cannot maintain safe control at low power. Give the towing provider the boat's size, propulsion, position, people aboard and damage concern.

Make an urgency or distress call when injury, flooding, fire, loss of control, grounding in dangerous conditions or immediate threat exists. The label matters less than clear facts and early communication.

Stopping propulsion is not the same as stopping the boat. Account for drift, traffic, depth and anchoring suitability using the anchor-system guide.

The dock inspection is not the final clearance

After recovery, do not run the damaged system on the trailer or at the dock unless the manufacturer procedure, cooling supply and safety controls make the test appropriate. Inspect the propeller, hub, shaft, skeg, gearcase oil, drain-plug magnets where specified, steering, mounts, transom, hull, bilges and alarms. Compare with pre-trip photographs and service records.

Propeller replacement alone may hide a bent shaft or damaged seal. Water or metal in gear lubricant, persistent vibration, new play, binding steering, cracking or leakage requires qualified diagnosis. Follow the exact engine, drive and boat manuals; torque values, removal sequences and inspection limits are model-specific.

Check current federal and state accident-reporting rules. U.S. requirements under 33 CFR Part 173 include specific triggers and timelines for deaths, injuries, disappearances, vessel loss and property damage. Use the current official text rather than an old threshold copied into a checklist.

Prevention is route discipline, not a stronger propeller

Use current charts and notices, respect aids to navigation, slow in unfamiliar or debris-prone water and maintain a lookout. Allow for changing water levels, submerged timber after storms and seasonal buoy changes. A chartplotter is not forward-looking sonar, and yesterday's track does not prove today's water is clear.

Stainless-steel and aluminum propellers transfer impact differently, but neither makes collision safe. Propeller choice belongs to the engine and boat application; it is not armor for shallow shortcuts. Carry the correct tools only if the operator knows the safe model-specific procedure, and secure a spare so it cannot become a projectile.

  • Power reduced and people checked
  • Life jackets, lookout and communication established
  • Bilges and water ingress inspected repeatedly

Before using propulsion

  • Engine disabled for visual inspection
  • No visible leak, mounting change or steering fault
  • Minimum-power stop rule briefed

After recovery

  • Position, symptoms and photographs saved
  • Model-specific inspection completed
  • Reporting and professional service needs resolved

Frequently asked questions

Can I keep boating after hitting a submerged object?

Do not resume normal operation because the engine still runs. Check people, flooding, steering, cooling and visible damage. Use only minimum power needed for immediate safety, and stop for vibration, noise, leakage, steering change or alarms.

Should I put the boat in reverse to free it from a log or rock?

Do not reverse hard over an unknown obstruction. Reduce power, protect people and stabilize the boat. The safe move depends on traffic, current, grounding and hull condition; communicate early when control is uncertain.

Can I inspect the propeller in the water?

From aboard, only after the engine is shut down and disabled against starting. Do not enter the water beside the boat merely to inspect; current, cold water, waves and unstable machinery can create a second emergency.

Why stop if the propeller only vibrates a little?

Vibration can indicate a bent blade, shaft, hub or internal damage and can compound seal, bearing, gear and mounting damage. Do not accelerate through it. Stop using the propulsion system and obtain qualified inspection.

Do I need to report hitting an underwater object?

Reporting depends on injury, disappearance, vessel loss, damage and jurisdiction-specific thresholds and timelines. Check the current U.S. Coast Guard and state rules rather than relying on an old dollar amount.

Plan with current information

Schedules, rules, prices and operating seasons change. Confirm the details that affect your trip directly with these primary sources:
